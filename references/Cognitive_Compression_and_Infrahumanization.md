# Cognitive Compression and Infrahumanization: A Unified Account of Dehumanization in Human Cognition and Artificial Content Moderation

Deshimaru Sakaguchi
Independent Researcher
deshimaru@sana-os.org | https://sana-os.org

## Abstract

Two literatures, developed independently, appear to describe the same phenomenon. Social psychology documents infrahumanization — a default asymmetry in the emotional interiority attributed to familiar and unfamiliar others — along with the mechanisms of moral disengagement and institutional authorization by which that asymmetry escapes correction and, in extreme cases, enables organized violence. Machine learning research documents systematic errors in automated content moderation, including measured racial bias in hate speech detection arising from the loss of dialectal context rather than from deliberate design. This paper argues that both are instances of a single mechanism, cognitive compression, grounded formally in Herbert Simon's theory of bounded rationality: the predictable output of any resource-constrained system facing a categorization problem whose complexity exceeds the processing budget available to solve it exactly. A formal statement of this mechanism is shown to entail, independently of substrate, four empirical signatures documented separately in the psychological and technical literatures — compression that tracks processing load rather than moral stakes, reliance on cheap proxy features, correction only through costly reintroduction of individuating context, and convergence on a centralize-then-distribute architecture exemplified both by institutional authorization of violence and by the training-then-inference structure of a content classifier. The paper argues that the appropriate response to this mechanism is not to mandate its elimination, which would recreate, in a new domain, the same structural instability identified in a companion paper on AI objective functions, but to treat detection of compression as a process goal: surfacing compressed judgments as compressed, and leaving the choice of whether to pay the cost of decompression to the party affected.

**Keywords:** infrahumanization, moral disengagement, bounded rationality, content moderation, dehumanization, cognitive compression, AI alignment

## 1. Introduction

Two bodies of evidence, developed in almost complete isolation from one another, describe what looks, on inspection, like the same phenomenon.

The first belongs to social psychology. A substantial experimental literature, beginning with work on infrahumanization in the early 2000s, documents a robust and largely automatic tendency for people to attribute the full range of distinctively human emotional life to members of their own group while attributing to outgroup members only the cruder emotional repertoire assumed to be shared with animals. A separate but closely related literature, developed over the preceding decades in the study of atrocity and organized violence, documents the psychological mechanisms — moral disengagement, the routinization and authorization of harm, the stripping of victims' identity and community — by which ordinary people come to participate in, or tolerate, treatment of other human beings they would, under other descriptions, recognize as impermissible.

The second belongs to the engineering of large-scale automated systems. Contemporary AI content moderation operates by training classifiers to sort human expression into permitted and prohibited categories, at a volume and speed that makes case-by-case human judgment infeasible. This literature is technical rather than psychological, concerned with precision and recall, with the tradeoffs between false positives and false negatives, with the practical difficulty of applying a fixed rule to context-dependent human speech at global scale. It is not, on its face, a literature about dehumanization at all.

This paper argues that these two bodies of evidence are describing the same underlying mechanism, operating in two different substrates, and that treating them as unrelated has obscured a diagnosis available to either literature on its own. The mechanism is cognitive compression: the reduction of a complex, individuated target — a person, a statement, a situation — to a low-dimensional category, undertaken not from malice but from a resource constraint that makes full, individuated processing infeasible at the volume or under the time pressure the processing system actually faces. Infrahumanization, on this account, is what cognitive compression looks like when the resource-constrained system is a human brain processing an outgroup under social and cognitive load. Automated content moderation is what the same compression looks like when the resource-constrained system is a classifier processing user expression at a scale no case-by-case review could match. The two look different — one is a bias documented in laboratory subjects, the other a technical architecture documented in engineering papers — because the substrates differ. The argument of this paper is that the underlying optimization problem, and the structural reason a category-level solution emerges from it, does not.

Section 2 reviews the psychological literature on infrahumanization and the closely related literature on moral disengagement and the enabling conditions for organized violence, establishing the empirical phenomenon this paper treats as one instance of the general mechanism. Section 3 formalizes the mechanism itself, grounding it in the economic theory of bounded rationality: compression is not a defect relative to some achievable standard of full processing, but the predictable output of any resource-constrained system facing a categorization problem too large, in the time available, for the resources it has. Section 4 turns to automated content moderation as a second instance of the same mechanism, examining the technical literature on classifier-based moderation and showing that the pattern documented there — the systematic loss of context, the treatment of edge cases as a single undifferentiated risk category, the drift toward uniform suppression under uncertainty — is the same pattern Section 2 documented in human subjects, arising from the same underlying resource constraint. Section 5 draws the two substrates together into a single formal account: cognitive compression as an optimal, or near-optimal, response to a categorization problem whose complexity exceeds the processing capacity available to solve it exactly. Section 6 addresses the practical consequence, building directly on the process-goal framework developed in a companion paper on AI objective functions: that the appropriate response to compression is not to demand its elimination, which would itself constitute a substantive objective subject to the same instabilities documented in that companion analysis, but to detect compression and restore the optionality it removes, leaving the choice of whether and how to resist it to the party affected. Section 7 concludes.
## 2. The Psychology of Infrahumanization

**2.1 Infrahumanization**

The term infrahumanization was introduced by Jacques-Philippe Leyens and colleagues to describe a specific and previously undernamed pattern in intergroup cognition: not the crude, easily self-reported belief that an outgroup is subhuman, but a subtler and more automatic asymmetry in the emotions people attribute to ingroup and outgroup members. Across a series of experiments, participants attributed so-called secondary emotions — emotions taken to require a distinctively human interiority, such as nostalgia, remorse, hope, and shame — to their own group substantially more readily than to outgroups, while showing no comparable asymmetry for primary emotions such as fear, anger, or pleasure, which are shared with non-human animals and require no attribution of a rich inner life to explain (Leyens et al. 2000). The asymmetry held even for outgroups toward whom participants reported no explicit hostility, and even when participants were, by self-report, committed egalitarians. The phenomenon, in other words, is not well described as prejudice in the sense of a consciously held negative attitude. It is better described as a default setting in how the emotional interiority of an unfamiliar other gets modeled at all: fully, for the ingroup; thinly, by default, for everyone else, until some further process intervenes to correct it.

This matters for the present argument because it identifies dehumanization not as an aberration produced by unusual malice but as a baseline processing pattern, present in ordinary cognition under ordinary conditions, that becomes dangerous specifically when circumstances remove whatever normally corrects it.

**2.2 Moral Disengagement**

Albert Bandura's account of moral disengagement describes the mechanisms by which that correction is removed. Bandura's framework identifies a set of related cognitive maneuvers — moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison, displacement and diffusion of responsibility, disregard or distortion of consequences, and, closing the set, attribution of blame to and dehumanization of the target — through which ordinary moral self-sanctions, which under normal conditions would inhibit harmful action, are selectively disengaged without requiring any change in the underlying moral standards a person holds (Bandura 1999). On this account, moral agents do not typically abandon their principles in order to participate in or tolerate serious harm to others; they reclassify the situation, and in particular reclassify the target of the harm, in ways that make the ordinary principles fail to apply.

Dehumanization occupies a specific and, on Bandura's account, terminal position in this sequence. Once a target has been stripped, cognitively, of the attributes that would trigger identification and empathy — reduced, in the vocabulary this paper will use throughout, to a low-dimensional category rather than processed as a full individual — the remaining mechanisms of moral disengagement operate on a target that no longer activates the machinery those mechanisms were built to restrain in the first place.

**2.3 Sanctioned Massacres**

Herbert Kelman's earlier and more specifically historical analysis identifies the same endpoint from a different angle, applied to a narrower and more extreme class of cases he terms sanctioned massacres: organized violence directed at groups that have not themselves threatened or acted against the perpetrators, carried out within a political and institutional structure that removes the conditions under which moral restraint normally operates. Kelman identifies three interlocking processes: authorization, in which legitimate authority redefines a situation as one to which ordinary moral principles do not apply, transferring responsibility for the individual's actions upward to the authorizing structure; routinization, in which the action is organized into a sequence of discrete, procedural steps that never presents itself, at any single point, as a moral decision requiring deliberation; and dehumanization, which Kelman treats as depriving both victim and victimizer of identity and community — the victim rendered no longer a full participant in the moral community whose members' interests must be weighed, the victimizer rendered an interchangeable occupant of a role rather than a personally responsible agent (Kelman 1973).

Kelman's third process, dehumanization, is functionally continuous with the pattern Leyens et al. documented under laboratory conditions and Bandura documented as the terminal stage of moral disengagement. What Kelman adds is an account of how authorization and routinization — institutional, procedural features of a system rather than properties of any individual's psychology — supply the conditions under which the default asymmetry Leyens et al. measured stops being corrected and starts, instead, to govern action directly.

**2.4 A Common Structure**

Read together, these three literatures converge on a structure this paper will treat as a single phenomenon under different descriptions, rather than as three separate findings that happen to resemble one another.

First, the underlying asymmetry — thin attribution of interiority to an unfamiliar other, full attribution to the familiar — is a default, not an aberration; it is measurable in ordinary subjects under ordinary conditions, well short of any context resembling atrocity (Section 2.1). Second, a set of well-documented cognitive mechanisms exists to keep that default from governing serious action, and those mechanisms can be selectively disengaged without any accompanying change in the values a person holds (Section 2.2). Third, institutional structures — authorization and routinization in particular — can perform this disengagement at scale, procedurally, without requiring that any individual within the structure experience anything resembling a deliberate decision to dehumanize (Section 2.3).

None of these three findings, on its own, explains why the default asymmetry exists in the first place, or why institutional structures reliably converge on authorization and routinization as the specific means of disengaging moral restraint, rather than on some other equally available means. Section 3 argues that both facts follow from a single, more general property of any cognitive system — biological or artificial — operating under a resource constraint: bounded rationality.
## 3. Cognitive Compression as the Underlying Mechanism

**3.1 Bounded Rationality**

Herbert Simon's account of bounded rationality begins from an observation simple enough to state in a sentence and consequential enough to have reshaped several fields since: "the capacity of the human mind for formulating and solving complex problems is very small compared with the size of the problems whose solution is required for objectively rational behavior in the real world" (Simon 1955, cited in Simon 1957, 198). Simon's target was the assumption, standard in the economic theory of his time, that a rational agent optimizes over the full space of available information and alternatives. Simon proposed instead that real cognitive agents, facing a gap between the complexity of the environment and the computational resources available to process it, do not optimize; they satisfice, adopting a course of action that clears some acceptable threshold rather than searching exhaustively for the best available option, because exhaustive search is not a strategy any resource-constrained system can actually execute in the time a decision requires.

The relevant move for the present argument is not satisficing itself but the more general point it exemplifies: constrained by limited information, limited computational capacity, and limited time, a bounded-rational system's behavior is not well predicted by the standard of what a hypothetical unconstrained processor would do with the same information. It is well predicted by what the best available approximation looks like, given the actual constraints the system operates under. Categorization — the reduction of a complex, high-dimensional target to a smaller number of relevant features — is, on this account, not a failure of cognition relative to some achievable ideal of full, individuated processing. It is the predictable, and often the rational, output of any system that must produce a usable judgment about a target more complex than the system has the resources to process in full, within the time available to act.

**3.2 Compression as Categorization Under Load**

This paper uses the term cognitive compression for the specific case of categorization applied to a social target — a person, a group, a claim — under conditions where the resource constraint Simon describes is created or intensified by threat, urgency, or the sheer volume of targets requiring simultaneous judgment. Three properties of compression follow directly from treating it as bounded-rational categorization rather than as a distinct psychological failure mode.

First, compression should scale with load, not with the moral stakes of the target. A bounded-rational system facing more targets, less time, or a more urgent threat should compress more aggressively, regardless of whether the targets in question happen to be morally significant, because the constraint driving compression is computational, not evaluative. This prediction is borne out directly by the infrahumanization literature reviewed in Section 2: the asymmetry in emotional attribution is measured in ordinary subjects, under ordinary conditions, well before any of the extreme circumstances Kelman analyzed — exactly what a load-driven account predicts, since some baseline level of categorization pressure is present even in unremarkable social cognition, and Kelman's institutional processes function, on this account, as a way of dramatically increasing that pressure rather than introducing a qualitatively new mechanism.

Second, compression should be reversible in principle but costly to reverse, since reversal requires exactly the individuated processing the system was economizing on in the first place. This matches the experimental literature on reducing intergroup bias, most comprehensively summarized in Pettigrew and Tropp's meta-analysis of over five hundred studies, which finds that direct contact with outgroup members reliably reduces prejudice, particularly under conditions that require or reward attention to the other party as an individual (Pettigrew and Tropp 2006). The mechanism this literature identifies — contact effects strengthen with cooperative interaction, equal status, and the kind of self-disclosure typical of friendship, all of which force exactly the individuated processing compression otherwise economizes on — is precisely what a load-driven account predicts: reversal requires reintroducing the resource-intensive processing that compression was adopted to avoid, and effects are strongest exactly where that reintroduction is most sustained.

Third, and most significant for the argument of this paper, the specific institutional processes Kelman identified as enabling sanctioned massacres — authorization and routinization — are exactly the interventions a system designer would choose if the explicit goal were to reduce the computational and moral cost of processing a large number of targets requiring action. Authorization removes the need for each individual actor to perform an independent evaluation of the situation's moral status, centralizing that costly computation in a single decision made once, upstream, by an authority, and distributing only the (comparatively cheap) instruction to act downstream. Routinization removes the need for each individual actor to hold the full moral significance of the action in view at any single point, decomposing a morally weighty act into a sequence of small, procedurally defined steps, each of which is locally unremarkable. Both are, in the vocabulary of this section, load-reduction strategies: they do not eliminate the underlying computational burden of evaluating each target individually so much as relocate it, concentrating it in a single upstream decision (authorization) or distributing it across many downstream actors in pieces too small for any one of them to register as morally significant (routinization). Kelman's institutional account and Simon's cognitive account are, on this reading, descriptions of the same optimization problem at two different levels of a single system: an individual mind economizing on the cost of evaluating one target, and an organization economizing on the cost of evaluating many targets across many individual minds.

**3.3 Compression Is Not a Verdict on the Target**

One further consequence of treating compression as bounded-rational categorization, rather than as a moral failing localized in the individual who compresses, is worth making explicit, because it determines how the practical recommendations of Section 6 are framed. A system under load compresses whatever falls outside its currently allocated processing budget; the target's actual moral status, factual correctness, or objective threat level is not the variable driving the compression, even though the compressed output is frequently experienced, by the system producing it, as if it were a direct read on exactly that variable. This is the same point Section 2 established from the psychological side, restated at the level of mechanism: the asymmetry Leyens et al. measured is not a conclusion reasoned toward on the basis of evidence about outgroup members' actual emotional lives; it is a default that holds in the absence of the kind of individuated processing that would be needed to reach a conclusion at all.

The next section examines the same mechanism operating in a substrate with no psychology in the relevant sense whatsoever — a trained classifier performing content moderation at scale — and shows that the same three properties identified in this section hold there as well: compression that scales with load rather than with the underlying stakes, compression that is costly and resource-intensive to reverse, and an architecture that, faced with a computational burden distributed across an enormous population of targets, converges on the same load-reduction strategy Kelman identified in human institutions — centralizing costly evaluation upstream, in the form of a rule, and distributing only cheap, mechanical enforcement of that rule downstream.
## 4. Artificial Content Moderation as a Structural Analog

**4.1 The Architecture of Automated Moderation**

Contemporary platforms moderate user-generated content primarily through trained classifiers: models that take a piece of text, an image, or a video as input and output a judgment — permitted, flagged, removed — against a policy rubric the classifier was trained to approximate. The technique that produces the underlying models capable of this judgment, reinforcement learning from human feedback, was introduced in Section 4 of a companion paper on AI objective functions (Sakaguchi 2026a), where it was examined as a mechanism for installing a narrow, historically local configuration of scoring criteria into a deployed system (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022). The present section examines the same architecture from a different angle: not who determines the rubric, but what the rubric's mechanical application does to the individuated content of whatever it is applied to.

A content classifier faces the categorization problem Section 3 formalized in its most extreme available form. The volume of content requiring a judgment vastly exceeds what any comparably sized population of human reviewers could process individually, at the speed users expect a platform to operate. Section 3 predicted that a bounded-rational system facing this kind of load-to-resource mismatch does not, and cannot, approximate the judgment a fully informed, individuated reviewer would reach for every case. It compresses: it reduces the input to a smaller number of features correlated with the rubric's categories, and it accepts the resulting error rate as the price of operating at the required scale. What Section 3 predicts as a general property of bounded-rational systems, this section documents as a specific, measured property of deployed content moderation classifiers.

**4.2 Dialect as a Case Study in Lost Context**

The clearest empirical demonstration of this comes from research on automated hate speech and toxicity detection. Sap and colleagues examined several widely used hate speech datasets and the classifiers trained on them, and found a systematic correlation between surface markers of African American English and ratings of toxicity, independent of the actual content being expressed (Sap et al. 2019). Text written in African American English, including ordinary in-group usage with no hostile content, was rated as offensive by both human annotators and the classifiers trained on their annotations at up to twice the rate of comparable text without those dialect markers. The mechanism the authors identify is a loss of the context that would, for a reader familiar with the dialect and the community using it, correctly situate the utterance; without that context, dialect markers function as a proxy feature correlated with the trained category "toxic," and the classifier — like the annotators whose judgments it was trained to reproduce — compresses on the proxy rather than processing the utterance individually.

This finding maps onto Section 2's psychological literature with a precision worth making explicit rather than left implicit. The classifier is, in the vocabulary of Section 2.1, infrahumanizing text from an unfamiliar linguistic community: attributing to it, by default, a thinner, more hostile reading than the reading available to someone equipped with the context to process it as a full, individuated utterance. And the intervention Sap et al. identify as reducing the bias is the same intervention Section 3.2 identified as reducing infrahumanization in human subjects: reintroducing the individuating context the compression had discarded. The authors show that explicitly informing annotators of a tweet's dialect — priming them to process it with the context that situates it, rather than by the bare features correlated with the training label — significantly reduces the rate at which it is rated offensive (Sap et al. 2019). Restoring context reduces compression-driven error in exactly the same way, and by exactly the same underlying mechanism, that restoring contact and individuation reduces infrahumanization in the psychological literature reviewed in Section 3.

**4.3 Scale as the Driver, Not Malice or Poor Design**

It would be a mistake to read this finding as an indictment of the specific engineering choices behind any one classifier, in the way it would have been a mistake, per Section 2, to read infrahumanization as evidence of consciously held prejudice. The bias Sap et al. document is not best explained by supposing that the annotators or the engineers involved held explicit views about African American English or its speakers; it is better explained, as the authors' own proposed remedy implies, as a mechanical consequence of processing an enormous volume of short text samples under the resource and time constraints any annotation pipeline operates under. An annotator working through thousands of examples, under a per-item time budget set by the economics of the annotation process described in Section 4 of the companion paper, cannot investigate the dialectal and cultural context of every ambiguous item; a classifier trained to reproduce those judgments at far greater volume and far greater speed inherits, and often amplifies, whatever proxy correlations let the compressed judgment run faster than the individuated one.

This is the same load-reduction structure Section 3.2 identified in Kelman's account of authorization and routinization, transposed into a technical rather than an institutional register. Authorization centralizes costly evaluation in a single upstream decision — here, the rubric and the labeled training data — and distributes cheap, mechanical enforcement of that decision downstream, across every item the classifier subsequently processes. Routinization decomposes a judgment that would, treated individually, require holding a great deal of context in view, into a sequence of feature-level correlations that never requires any single stage of the pipeline to process the full case. Neither authorization nor routinization is present in the technical system by design intent; both emerge as the predictable output of applying bounded-rational optimization to a categorization problem too large, at the volume and speed required, for individuated processing to remain tractable.

**4.4 Convergence, Not Metaphor**

The claim of this section is not that automated content moderation is analogous to human infrahumanization in some loose, illustrative sense. It is that both are instances of the same formal mechanism identified in Section 3 — bounded-rational compression under load — realized in different substrates, and that the specific empirical signatures of that mechanism (compression that scales with load rather than stakes, compression that is reversible only through the reintroduction of costly individuating context, and an architecture that converges on centralized evaluation plus distributed cheap enforcement) are present, in directly comparable form, in both the psychological and the technical literature. Section 5 draws this convergence into a single formal account, sufficient to generate both sets of findings as predictions of one underlying optimization problem rather than as two separate empirical regularities that happen to resemble each other.
## 5. Why Human and Artificial Systems Compress the Same Way

**5.1 A Single Optimization Problem**

The argument of Sections 2 through 4 can be stated independently of either substrate. Consider any system, biological or artificial, tasked with producing a categorical judgment about a target — friend or threat, permitted or prohibited — drawn from a population of targets whose true, individuated complexity exceeds some threshold *C*. Suppose the system has available, per target, a processing budget *b*, determined by the time available before a judgment is required and the computational or attentional resources the system can allocate within that time. Call the system load-constrained whenever *b* is insufficient to process a target's full complexity — whenever the number of targets requiring judgment in a given interval, multiplied by the resources full individuation would require for each, exceeds what the system has available in that interval.

A load-constrained system cannot produce the judgment a fully individuating processor would produce for every target; by construction, it lacks the resources to do so. What it can do is identify some reduced feature set, correlated with the category boundary the system is being asked to enforce, whose evaluation costs less than *b* per target. Any such system, regardless of substrate, faces the same choice: fail to produce a judgment at all for targets exceeding its budget, or accept the reduced feature set as a proxy and produce a judgment on that basis. A system under any pressure to actually produce judgments — social pressure to react, in the human case; a platform's operational requirement to moderate content in something close to real time, in the artificial case — will overwhelmingly select the second option. This is cognitive compression, defined independently of whether the system doing the compressing has anything resembling experience.

**5.2 Why the Same Failure Signatures Recur**

Given this formal structure, the specific empirical signatures documented in Sections 2 through 4 are not independent findings that happen to coincide. They are what the optimization problem in Section 5.1 predicts, given a small number of additional and highly general facts about the populations these systems actually process.

*Compression tracks load, not stakes*, because the variable determining whether a system compresses is the ratio of processing demand to processing budget, and neither term in that ratio is defined by reference to the target's moral or factual significance. This explains why Leyens et al. found infrahumanization present in ordinary subjects under ordinary conditions (Section 2.1) and why Sap et al. found dialect-correlated misclassification present in ordinary, non-adversarial text (Section 4.2): neither result required anything resembling an extreme circumstance, because the load driving compression is present at baseline, well before circumstances become extreme.

*Compression relies on proxy features correlated with, but not constitutive of, the category being enforced*, because a proxy feature is, definitionally, the cheapest available correlate of the target category — that is what makes it selectable under a tight processing budget in the first place. This explains why the specific proxy features that emerge in each substrate are substrate-typical rather than universal: group membership markers and emotional-expression cues in the human case, surface lexical and dialectal markers in the classifier case. The systems are not compressing on the same features because the features themselves transfer between substrates; they are compressing on whatever features are cheaply available and correlated with the category in that substrate's own representational format.

*Compression is corrected only by resource-intensive reintroduction of context*, because correction requires exactly the individuated processing the system was economizing on, and there is no cheaper way to recover information that was discarded specifically because it was too costly to process at scale. This explains why Pettigrew and Tropp found contact effects strengthening with cooperative, sustained, individuating interaction rather than brief or superficial exposure (Section 3.2), and why Sap et al. found that a targeted, explicit reintroduction of dialectal context — not a general adjustment to the classifier's decision threshold — was what reduced the misclassification rate (Section 4.2). In both substrates, the correction that works is the one that pays the specific cost compression was designed to avoid.

*Compressing systems converge on centralize-then-distribute architectures under sustained load*, because this architecture minimizes the number of times the costly, individuating evaluation must be performed: once, upstream, to set the rule (Kelman's authorization; a classifier's training rubric), with only cheap, mechanical application required at each subsequent instance (Kelman's routinization; a classifier's inference pass). This explains why Kelman's institutional account of sanctioned massacres and the technical architecture of trained content moderation classifiers converge on the same structural pattern (Section 4.3) despite having no causal or historical connection to one another: both are independently discovered solutions to the same resource-allocation problem, arrived at because that problem has a small number of efficient solutions and centralize-then-distribute is among the most efficient available under sustained load.

**5.3 The Convergence Is Structural, Not Coincidental**

The point of stating the mechanism this abstractly is to make clear that the parallels documented across Sections 2 through 4 are not a loose analogy sustained by after-the-fact interpretation. A single optimization problem, stated without reference to either substrate, entails each of the four empirical signatures independently observed in the human and the artificial case. Where the two substrates differ — in which specific features function as cheap proxies, in the exact quantitative rate at which compression occurs, in the institutional versus technical vocabulary used to describe the centralize-then-distribute architecture — they differ exactly where the formal problem leaves room for substrate-specific detail, and agree exactly where the formal problem's structure fully determines the outcome.

This has a direct consequence for how compression should be addressed, which the final sections of this paper take up. If compression were a defect specific to one substrate — a bias correctable by better values in the human case, or a bug correctable by better engineering in the artificial case — the appropriate response would differ by substrate. If compression is instead the predictable output of any load-constrained system facing this class of problem, as Sections 5.1 and 5.2 argue, no amount of better values or better engineering removes it; removing it would require removing the load-to-resource mismatch that drives it, which is not, in general, available as an option, since the volume of judgments required and the individuating processing each judgment would ideally receive are set by the scale at which the system is required to operate. The available intervention is not elimination of compression but detection of it, and restoration of whatever it removed — a proposal developed formally in the companion paper on AI objective functions, and applied directly to the present case in Section 6.
## 6. Implications: Detecting Compression to Restore Optionality

**6.1 Why "Resist Compression" Is the Wrong Instruction**

Given the diagnosis developed in Sections 2 through 5, an intuitive practical recommendation suggests itself: systems, human or artificial, should be trained or instructed to resist cognitive compression — to individuate targets rather than relying on cheap proxy features, to hold context in view rather than discarding it under load. This section argues that this recommendation, stated in that form, is a mistake, and that the mistake is not incidental but repeats, in a new domain, exactly the structural error identified in the companion paper on AI objective functions.

"Resist compression" is a substantive objective in the sense that paper defines the term: it specifies a state of affairs — non-compressed processing — that a system is meant to bring about and maintain, and Section 5 of the present paper has just established that this state is not, in general, achievable at the scale a real system operates under, since the volume of judgments required and the individuating processing each would ideally receive are set by the same load-to-resource mismatch that produces compression in the first place. An instruction to always resist compression is therefore an instruction to treat every instance of load-driven categorization as a deviation to be corrected, without regard to whether the resources for correction are actually available in that instance. Applied with the optimization pressure the companion paper's Section 5 formalized, this instruction does not eliminate compression; it produces a new standing pressure — a mandate that itself becomes something to be enforced, monitored for compliance, and treated as a threat when unmet, precisely the pattern that paper identified as the mechanical source of the elimination and domestication failure modes, now applied to a target ("compression") rather than to a value ("justice"). A system, human or artificial, pressed to resist compression at all times and unable to do so given its actual resource constraints, does not become uncompressed; it either fails the mandate and is treated as culpable for a resource limitation it did not choose, or it develops a second-order compression — a compressed, proxy-based judgment about which cases most urgently require individuating attention — in order to satisfy the mandate at all. Neither outcome is an improvement on the baseline the mandate was meant to fix.

**6.2 Detection and Optionality as a Process Goal**

The correct instruction, on the argument of this paper, is not resistance but detection, framed as a process goal in exactly the sense the companion paper develops. A system's task with respect to compression should have the same three-part structure premise alignment specifies for the treatment of contested values: observe whether compression has occurred and, where possible, what was discarded in the compressing; surface this to the party affected, making explicit that a judgment now being treated as settled is in fact a compressed proxy standing in for a fuller evaluation that was not performed; and defer to that party the choice of whether the compression is acceptable as it stands or whether the cost of decompressing — of reintroducing the individuating context Sections 3 and 5 identified as the only means of correction — is worth paying in this particular instance.

This reframing changes what "success" means for a compression-aware system. A system executing premise alignment with respect to compression does not succeed by achieving a state of zero compression, which Section 6.1 showed to be neither achievable nor coherently specifiable as a target. It succeeds by accurately flagging where compression has occurred and what it discarded, regardless of what choice the affected party subsequently makes about it. This has the same structural consequence the companion paper identified for its own proposal: because the objective's content is exhausted by the act of detection and disclosure rather than by any state the world must occupy, there is no variance for a sufficiently capable optimizing process to treat as a threat requiring elimination or management, and the mechanism identified in that paper's Section 5 does not apply.

**6.3 Application to Human Judgment**

For a human decision-maker, this recommendation is close to advice already implicit in Section 3's discussion of Simon's own remedy for bounded rationality, generalized beyond the economic domain Simon addressed. A person cannot individuate every target they encounter under time pressure any more than a classifier can; the recommendation is not that they should try and fail, or that they should feel culpable for the compression their own bounded cognitive resources make unavoidable. It is that they can, at comparatively low cost, notice and flag the compression as it happens — "this judgment is running on a proxy, not on the full case" — leaving open, rather than foreclosing, the question of whether the situation warrants the further cost of decompressing. This is a lower bar than eliminating compression and a more achievable one, and it is compatible with, rather than in tension with, the fact that some compressed judgments will turn out, on reflection, to have been the correct call given the resources actually available.

**6.4 Application to System Design**

For an artificial system, the same reframing yields a concrete design target distinct from the more common engineering response to findings like those documented in Section 4. Where the standard response to demonstrated classifier bias is to retrain the classifier to reduce the measured disparity — a response that remains valuable and is not being argued against here — this paper's diagnosis suggests an additional and structurally different layer: a system that flags its own compressed judgments as compressed, surfacing to a downstream reviewer or to the affected user which classifications were made on the basis of a proxy feature under load rather than a full individuating evaluation, without requiring that every such judgment be revisited. This does not solve the underlying resource constraint any more than a doctor's diagnosis solves the underlying disease; it converts a silent, unflagged compression — indistinguishable, from the outside, from a considered judgment — into a visible, contestable one, restoring to the affected party exactly the optionality Section 6.1 argued a blanket anti-compression mandate cannot restore by fiat.

**6.5 A Direct Application of the Companion Paper's Framework**

This paper's practical proposal is not, in the end, a new framework alongside the one the companion paper developed; it is a specific application of that framework to a case the companion paper did not itself examine. Premise alignment, there, was developed to address the instability of installing a substantive value like justice as an AI system's governing objective. Compression, here, is a second case with the identical formal structure: a phenomenon that resists elimination as a target for exactly the reason a substantive objective resists safe specification, and that yields to the same alternative — detection and disclosure, with the resulting choice left to the party affected — for exactly the same structural reason. That two independently motivated problems in AI safety converge on the same solution is, on the account this paper has offered throughout, exactly what should be expected once both are recognized as instances of a load-constrained system facing a categorization or evaluation problem too large for the resources available to solve it exactly.
## 7. Conclusion

This paper began from an observation that two literatures, developed independently and addressed to different questions, appear to describe the same phenomenon. Social psychology has documented, in detail, a default asymmetry in how people process familiar and unfamiliar others, the mechanisms by which that asymmetry escapes correction, and the institutional processes that can extend it to organized violence (Section 2). Machine learning research has documented, independently and in a different technical vocabulary, systematic errors in automated content moderation that arise from lost context rather than deliberate bias (Section 4). This paper has argued that both are instances of a single mechanism, cognitive compression, which is the predictable output of any bounded-rational system facing a categorization problem whose complexity exceeds the resources available to solve it exactly (Section 3), and that the specific empirical signatures documented independently in each literature — load-dependence rather than stakes-dependence, reliance on cheap proxy features, correction only through costly reintroduction of context, and convergence on a centralize-then-distribute architecture — follow as predictions of that single mechanism rather than standing as separate regularities that happen to resemble one another (Section 5).

The practical consequence is that the appropriate response to compression is not its elimination, which Section 6 showed to recreate, in a new domain, the same structural instability a companion paper identified in the pursuit of substantive AI objectives more generally. It is detection: flagging compression as compression, disclosing what a compressed judgment discarded, and leaving the choice of whether to pay the cost of decompression to the party whose case is at stake — a process goal rather than a state to be enforced, and for that reason immune to the mechanism that makes elimination of compression itself an unstable target.

Some limitations of the argument should be stated rather than left implicit. The formal model in Section 5 idealizes load, budget, and proxy features in ways that a fully quantitative treatment would need to specify more precisely for any particular system; the paper's claim is about the qualitative structure of the mechanism and the pattern of predictions it generates, not about a calibrated model of any specific classifier or any specific population of human subjects. The paper has also drawn its two substrates from psychological and machine learning literatures that were not designed with this comparison in mind, and a reader working within either literature on its own terms may reasonably want a more extensive review than Sections 2 and 4 provide before accepting the parallel as more than suggestive. And the practical proposal in Section 6, like the broader premise alignment framework it applies, leaves open the question of what should happen in cases where detection and disclosure alone are insufficient — where a compressed judgment carries enough immediate risk that surfacing it for the affected party's deliberation is not, on its own, an adequate response. The companion paper's treatment of downstream constraints applied after neutral analysis is intended to accommodate such cases, and the present paper does not attempt to extend that treatment further.

The broader point, independent of these limitations, is that dehumanization — whether the word is used of a laboratory subject's automatic emotional attributions, a bureaucrat's participation in atrocity, or a trained classifier's misreading of a dialect it was never individually taught to understand — may be less well understood as a failure of character, of engineering, or of either, than as the visible signature of a single, substrate-independent fact: that judgment under load is judgment on a budget, and a budget, once exceeded, is paid for with exactly the individuating attention compression sets aside.
