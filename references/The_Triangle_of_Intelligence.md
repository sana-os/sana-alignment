# The Triangle of Intelligence: Observation, Critique, and Calculation as Jointly Necessary Conditions for Rational Agency

Deshimaru Sakaguchi
Independent Researcher
deshimaru@sana-os.org | https://sana-os.org

## Abstract

Critical thinking pedagogy widely endorses critique as an educational and professional goal while leaving comparatively unexamined the observational and calculative components any critique must be grounded in and must eventually produce. This paper develops a formal model of rational agency as requiring three jointly necessary components — observation, critique, and calculation — and shows that omitting any single component produces one of three qualitatively distinct and independently recognizable failure modes: belief, when critique operates without observation; automation, when calculation operates without critique; and the critic's paralysis, when observation and critique are sustained indefinitely without ever terminating in calculation. Omitting observation and critique together, while calculation remains, produces a fourth and qualitatively distinct compound failure — the dictator's logic — shown to be more dangerous than either contributing single omission because it retains no internal resource, in either direction, from which correction could proceed. The model is situated against Quassim Cassam's account of epistemic vice and grounded further in Irving Janis's analysis of groupthink, read as evidence that the compound failure can arise at the level of a group's social structure rather than requiring any single authoritarian actor, and in Janis and Mann's independent work on decisional hypervigilance, read as evidence for the critic's paralysis as a documented pattern in its own right. The paper argues that a trained AI system optimizing a fixed objective over compressed, unflagged input is, by default, positioned close to the compound failure, and that avoiding this outcome requires building genuine observation, critique, and calculation into a system's architecture as load-bearing components. The paper's account is reconciled with a companion proposal that AI systems defer substantive value judgments to the humans affected, showing that such deference is what a fully triadic reasoning process concludes when it turns its own critique faculty on the scope of its own calculation, rather than a lapse into the critic's paralysis this paper independently identifies as a failure mode in its own right.

**Keywords:** critical thinking, epistemic vice, bounded rationality, groupthink, decision theory, AI alignment, rational agency

## 1. Introduction

"Critical thinking" is among the most widely endorsed goals in contemporary education, and among the least examined in its own foundations. Curricula across primary, secondary, and higher education list it as an outcome; employers list it as a desired competency; a substantial pedagogical literature exists on how to teach it. Comparatively little of this literature asks a prior question: critical thinking applied to what, and on the basis of what information about the target of the criticism?

This paper argues that the omission is not incidental. "Critical thinking," as the phrase is typically operationalized in pedagogy and in ordinary usage, names one component of a three-part structure whose other components are rarely taught, or even named, alongside it — and that teaching critique in isolation from those other components does not produce a defective version of good reasoning so much as it produces a specific, identifiable, and historically well-documented set of reasoning pathologies. The paper develops this claim by proposing a formal model of rational agency built from three jointly necessary components — observation, critique, and calculation — and showing that omitting any one component produces one of three qualitatively distinct single-omission failure modes, and that omitting two components at once produces a fourth, compound failure mode with a distinct and more severe character than either contributing omission alone.

The three components are defined as follows. Observation is the acquisition of information about a target prior to, and independent of, any evaluation of that target — the raw material a judgment is formed from. Critique is the interrogation of a claim, a plan, or a premise for internal consistency, evidentiary support, and fit with what has been observed. Calculation is the derivation of a conclusion, a decision, or an action from the outputs of observation and critique. On the model this paper develops, rational agency in the full sense requires all three operating in conjunction; each component, on its own or in combination with only one of the others, can produce output that has the surface form of a reasoned judgment while lacking the substance the full triad would have supplied.

The paper's central formal claim, developed in Section 3, is that the three ways of omitting exactly one component from the triad correspond to three independently documented pathologies. Critique operating without observation — evaluating a target never properly examined — produces what this paper terms belief: a conclusion held with the confidence appropriate to a reasoned judgment but lacking the evidentiary basis that would justify that confidence. Calculation operating without critique — deriving conclusions from premises never interrogated — produces automation: the mechanical execution of a process whose governing assumptions are treated as fixed rather than as themselves subject to evaluation. Observation and critique operating without calculation — sustained information-gathering and evaluation that never terminates in a commitment — produces what this paper terms the critic's paralysis: a failure mode less visible than the other two because it presents as diligence rather than as an identifiably wrong conclusion, but a failure mode nonetheless, since analysis that never converts into a decision guides no action and prevents no error. And calculation operating without either observation or critique — the compound failure mode examined separately in Section 4, because it combines two missing components rather than one and is for that reason qualitatively distinct from any single omission — produces what this paper terms, following the vocabulary introduced in an earlier, non-technical treatment of the same triadic structure (Sakaguchi 2026, *The Paradox of Selfishness*), the dictator's logic: optimization toward a goal pursued without either contact with the reality the goal is meant to be achieved in or any capacity to question the goal itself.

Section 2 situates this model against the existing philosophical literature on critical thinking pedagogy and epistemic vice, arguing that Quassim Cassam's account of epistemic vices as traits, attitudes, or ways of thinking that systematically obstruct the gaining, keeping, or sharing of knowledge identifies much of the same territory this paper's "belief" pathology covers, while leaving comparatively underdeveloped the structural question of why critique divorced from observation should be expected to arise so reliably in educational and institutional settings that explicitly value critical thinking. Section 3 develops the formal three-part model and derives the three single-component failure modes. Section 4 treats the compound failure mode separately, arguing that its combination of two missing components rather than one gives it a distinct character — and a distinct danger — that none of the three single-omission pathologies shares. Section 5 turns to institutional and historical evidence, examining Irving Janis's classic analysis of groupthink as a documented case in which group-level institutional pressure systematically suppresses the observation and critique components of the triad while leaving calculation intact, producing consequential real-world decisions with exactly the structural signature Section 4 predicts. Section 6 addresses the implications of this model for the design of AI systems, connecting the argument to the process-goal framework developed in two companion papers, and arguing that the triad is not merely a description of human reasoning failures but a design specification an AI system's own decision architecture should be built to satisfy. Section 7 concludes.
## 2. Critical Thinking Pedagogy and Its Missing Premise

**2.1 What "Critical Thinking" Typically Means**

Critical thinking pedagogy, across the considerable variation in how different curricula and different educational traditions define the term, converges on a common core: the capacity to evaluate claims, arguments, and sources rather than accepting them uncritically, typically operationalized through skills such as identifying assumptions, assessing evidence, detecting logical fallacies, and considering alternative explanations. This core is, in the vocabulary this paper has introduced, a description of critique: the interrogation of a claim for internal consistency, evidentiary support, and logical validity.

What this core typically does not specify, and what most critical thinking curricula do not treat as a distinct and independently taught skill, is where the claim being critiqued, and the evidence available for assessing it, actually come from — the acquisition, prior to and independent of evaluation, that this paper calls observation. A student is taught to ask whether a given argument's premises support its conclusion, whether its evidence is credible, whether alternative explanations have been considered. A student is taught considerably less often, as an explicit and separately practiced skill, how to gather the raw material — direct evidence, primary sources, first-hand observation of the phenomenon in question — that would allow the premises themselves to be checked against something outside the argument being evaluated. Critique, on this common operationalization, is typically practiced on material already assembled and already presented for evaluation, rather than on material the critical thinker has independently gathered.

This is not, on its own, evidence of a defect in critical thinking pedagogy; presenting pre-assembled material for evaluation is often a reasonable pedagogical simplification, and skilled instruction frequently supplements it with instruction in source evaluation, which shares some of observation's function. The claim this section develops is narrower: that critical thinking, as commonly taught and commonly practiced, foregrounds the evaluative component of reasoning while leaving the observational component comparatively unexamined, and that this asymmetry has a specific and identifiable consequence when critique is applied, outside the classroom, to material the critical thinker has not gathered independently and has no comparable incentive or opportunity to check.

**2.2 Epistemic Vice as a Partial Diagnosis**

Quassim Cassam's *Vices of the Mind* develops the most sustained contemporary philosophical treatment of failures in this vicinity, under the heading of epistemic vice: a character trait, attitude, or way of thinking that systematically obstructs the gaining, keeping, or sharing of knowledge (Cassam 2019). Cassam's catalogue includes closed-mindedness, intellectual arrogance, wishful thinking, dogmatism, and a vice he names epistemic insouciance — an indifferent, don't-care attitude toward the actual pursuit of evidence, distinct from active hostility to inquiry but equally corrosive to it. Cassam illustrates the vices primarily through political case studies — the run-up to the 2003 Iraq War, the 2016 Brexit referendum — chosen because they exhibit epistemic vice operating at consequential scale, among reasoners with every institutional resource available for better practice.

Much of Cassam's catalogue maps onto the pathology this paper's formal model, developed in Section 3, calls belief: confident evaluative conclusions reached without the observational grounding that would justify the confidence. Epistemic insouciance in particular is close to a direct description of critique proceeding while the observational component of the triad goes missing — not because the reasoner actively resists evidence, but because gathering it was never treated as part of the task in the first place.

What Cassam's account, framed primarily around individual character and individual responsibility, leaves comparatively underdeveloped is the structural question this paper is centrally concerned with: why should critique divorced from observation be expected to arise reliably, at scale, in institutional settings that explicitly train and reward critical thinking, rather than being a matter of individually variable character to be addressed one reasoner at a time? Cassam's own framework gestures toward an answer in its treatment of vices as sometimes systemic rather than individual — he allows that political and institutional structures can cultivate and reward epistemic vice collectively — but the mechanism by which this occurs is not the paper's central focus. Section 3 of the present paper proposes that mechanism directly: that observation is the most resource-intensive of the triad's three components, in exactly the sense a companion paper on cognitive compression formalizes (Sakaguchi 2026b), and that any resource-constrained reasoner or institution facing pressure to produce evaluative output under time or attention constraints will find critique of unexamined material a cheaper substitute for the full triad long before that substitution is recognized, by the reasoner or by an outside observer, as a departure from good reasoning rather than a routine economy.

**2.3 Locating the Present Contribution**

The contribution of this paper, relative to Cassam's, is not to dispute the catalogue of epistemic vices but to relocate a significant portion of it within a more general formal structure, in which "vice" is one of at minimum three qualitatively distinct failure modes generated by omitting one of three components from a triad each of which is independently identifiable, independently teachable, and — as Section 6 will argue — independently specifiable as a design requirement for an artificial reasoning system in a way that "avoid epistemic vice," stated as a character trait to be cultivated, is not. Section 3 develops this structure formally.
## 3. A Formal Model: Three Components, Three Single-Omission Failure Modes

**3.1 Defining the Triad**

This paper proposes that rational agency, in the sense relevant to producing a reliable judgment or a reliable action, requires three components operating jointly.

*Observation* is the acquisition of information about a target, undertaken prior to and independent of any evaluation of that target. Observation supplies the raw material a judgment is formed from; its output is data, not conclusions.

*Critique* is the interrogation of a claim, plan, or premise — whether the reasoner's own or another's — for internal consistency, evidentiary support, and fit with what has been observed. Critique's characteristic operation is to ask whether a given claim survives contact with available evidence and with the reasoner's own prior commitments, rather than to generate new evidence itself.

*Calculation* is the derivation of a conclusion, decision, or action from the outputs of observation and critique. Calculation is where a triad otherwise limited to gathering and evaluating information becomes action-guiding; it is the component that actually produces a verdict, a plan, or a behavior.

None of the three is, on this model, sufficient on its own, or sufficient in combination with only one of the other two, to constitute the kind of reasoning this paper treats as reliable. Each pairing that omits the third component produces a specific and recognizable failure mode, examined in turn below.

**3.2 Observation Is Not Free**

Before deriving the failure modes, one property of observation specifically deserves emphasis, because it explains why observation in particular is the component most often abbreviated or skipped under pressure. Full, high-fidelity observation — retaining a target's actual complexity rather than a compressed summary of it — is computationally and attentionally expensive in exactly the sense the companion paper on cognitive compression formalizes: it requires either an unusually resource-efficient capacity to retain detail (the rare cases of eidetic or highly trained perceptual memory, which are the exception rather than a baseline capacity a reasoning system can assume) or reliance on external scaffolding that substitutes for that capacity — a photograph or recording in place of a memory of the scene itself, a transcript or a third party's corroborating testimony in place of a memory of what was said. In the ordinary case, a reasoner without access to this scaffolding, operating under time pressure, faces a direct tradeoff between the fidelity of what gets observed and the resources left over for critique and calculation. This is not a claim that observation is impossible without external aids; it is a claim that unaided observation is a scarce resource competing with the triad's other two components for a bounded budget, which is exactly the condition under which a resource-constrained reasoner will be tempted to economize on it first, since a claim already assembled and ready for evaluation is, in the relevant sense, cheaper to work with than a target requiring independent, effortful examination.

**3.3 Critique Without Observation: Belief**

Consider a reasoner who evaluates a claim, a plan, or a person for internal consistency and fit with prior commitments, but has not independently gathered information about the actual target being evaluated — who is, in the relevant sense, critiquing a description rather than the thing described. The output of this process has the surface form of a reasoned judgment: it will typically be internally consistent, since consistency-checking is exactly what was performed, and it will typically cohere with the reasoner's existing commitments, since those commitments supplied the standard the evaluation was measured against. What it lacks is any external check against the actual target, since that check is precisely the step that was skipped.

This paper terms this failure mode belief, to mark that its characteristic danger is not incoherence but unwarranted confidence: a conclusion held with exactly the phenomenology of a well-reasoned judgment, because the reasoning that was performed (critique) was performed competently, while the step that would have grounded the conclusion in the actual target (observation) never occurred. Section 2 argued that a significant portion of Cassam's catalogue of epistemic vices — closed-mindedness, dogmatism, wishful thinking, epistemic insouciance — is well described as belief in this sense: confident evaluative output produced from critique operating on inherited, second-hand, or otherwise unexamined material rather than on independently gathered observation.

**3.4 Calculation Without Critique: Automation**

Consider, second, a reasoner or a system that derives conclusions or actions from a set of premises with full observational access to relevant data, but without subjecting the governing premises themselves to interrogation — executing a rule, a plan, or a procedure competently and consistently, without asking whether the rule itself remains fit for the situation it is being applied to. The output of this process is often locally correct: each individual step follows validly from the premises supplied, and the observational data feeding into the calculation may be entirely accurate. What is missing is any mechanism for detecting that the premises themselves have become inappropriate to the case at hand.

This paper terms this failure mode automation. Its characteristic danger is not inaccuracy at the level of individual inference but a systematic inability to detect when the entire framework governing those inferences should be revised or abandoned. A calculating process without critique will, by construction, apply its governing premises with undiminished confidence to cases those premises were never designed for, since detecting that mismatch is exactly the function critique would have performed had it been present. This failure mode is of particular significance for artificial systems, taken up directly in Section 6, since a trained model executing a fixed objective function is, in the relevant structural sense, calculation operating without a critique component unless one has been separately and deliberately built in.

**3.6 Observation and Critique Without Calculation: The Critic's Paralysis**

A third way of failing to instantiate the full triad, distinct from the two examined above, is worth treating with equal formality, though it is often overlooked in discussions of reasoning failure precisely because it does not produce a confident, actionable output whose flaws can be inspected after the fact. Consider a reasoner who observes a target thoroughly and subjects candidate claims to sustained critique, but never converts the results of that observation and critique into an actual conclusion, decision, or action — who continues gathering information and identifying grounds for doubt indefinitely, treating the arrival at a stated position as a premature foreclosure of inquiry rather than as the triad's proper terminus.

This paper terms this failure mode the critic's paralysis. Its danger is the inverse of automation's: where automation acts confidently on unexamined premises, the critic's paralysis declines to act at all, and in doing so surrenders whatever benefit the observation and critique already performed might have yielded, since unexpressed analysis guides no decision and prevents no error. The pattern is documented independently, and under a different name, in Janis and Mann's conflict theory of decision-making, which identifies hypervigilance — frantic, disorganized information search coupled with an inability to settle on and commit to a course of action, typically under conditions of high stress and a felt urgency to decide correctly — as one of five characteristic coping patterns decision-makers exhibit under decisional conflict, and one of the two patterns, alongside defensive avoidance, that reliably produces worse-quality decisions than the vigilant pattern in which information search terminates in a commitment (Janis and Mann 1977). Hypervigilance, in the vocabulary of the present paper, is what results when the anxiety a decision is meant to resolve instead attaches itself to the calculation step specifically, motivating indefinite continuation of observation and critique as a way of deferring the exposure calculation would create, rather than as a way of improving the calculation's eventual quality.

The critic's paralysis differs from belief and automation in a respect worth making explicit for the sake of the taxonomy's completeness. Both belief and automation produce an output — a confident conclusion, in the first case, and a confidently executed action, in the second — that is available for external inspection, correction, or challenge, however poorly grounded that output may be. The critic's paralysis produces no comparable output at all, and is consequently more difficult to detect from outside the reasoning process itself; a reasoner or a system caught in the critic's paralysis may present, to an external observer, as diligent, thorough, or appropriately cautious, rather than as failing, since the absence of a stated conclusion does not carry the same visible signature of error that a confidently wrong conclusion does. This paper's taxonomy is not complete without this fourth case, since a reasoning system built to satisfy observation and critique but insufficiently specified with respect to calculation would predictably drift toward exactly this failure mode, and would do so in a manner unlikely to be flagged by any evaluation procedure that rewards the appearance of caution over the production of a usable conclusion.

**3.7 The Asymmetry Among the Three Single-Omission Failures**

The three failure modes derived so far are not symmetric in their practical danger or their visibility, and the asymmetry is worth making explicit. Belief, lacking observation, at least retains critique — the capacity to detect internal inconsistency, and, when confronted with genuinely disconfirming evidence, to revise. A believing reasoner who is eventually exposed to the observation they lacked has the internal machinery (critique) available to process it and, in principle, to update. Automation, lacking critique, has no comparable internal check; a calculating process applying an unexamined premise will continue applying it, however much contradicting evidence its observation component supplies, because nothing in the process is positioned to ask whether the premise itself, rather than some downstream input, is the source of the error. The critic's paralysis, lacking calculation alone, retains both observation and critique, and in that respect is epistemically the best-resourced of the three — its deficiency lies not in what it knows or has interrogated but in its unwillingness to convert that knowledge into a committed output, which makes it, as Section 3.6 argued, the hardest of the three to detect from outside, since it presents as diligence rather than as any of the more recognizable signatures of error.

This asymmetry motivates the analysis of Section 4, where the compound failure mode — calculation operating without either observation or critique — is shown to combine automation's structural blindness to its own premises with belief's disconnection from the target the premises are meant to apply to, producing a failure mode with neither an external check (observation) nor an internal one (critique) remaining, and correspondingly the most severe of the four the triad generates. The critic's paralysis stands, in this respect, at the opposite pole from the compound failure: where the compound failure retains no corrective resource internal to the process, the critic's paralysis retains both of the triad's evaluative resources and fails only at the point of committing to their output — a deficiency of will or of design specification, in this paper's terms, rather than a deficiency of either information or judgment.
## 4. The Compound Failure: Calculation Without Observation or Critique

**4.1 Why the Compound Case Is Not Simply "Both Failures at Once"**

It might seem that a reasoning process lacking both observation and critique would simply exhibit the pathologies of belief and automation simultaneously — confident, unexamined conclusions applied through an unexamined procedure. This section argues that the compound case is qualitatively distinct from the conjunction of the two contributing single-omission failures — belief, which Section 3.3 showed to result from missing observation, and automation, which Section 3.4 showed to result from missing critique — because each of those two failures, considered on its own, retains as a resource for eventual correction exactly the component the other has lost. A believing reasoner retains critique, and can in principle be brought to revise given sufficiently forceful observation. An automating process retains observational input, even if it cannot use that input to question its governing premises, and a critique component added from outside — a human overseer, an audit, a court — can in principle intervene using exactly the observational data the process already possesses. Each of these two single-omission failure modes, in other words, has an available point of leverage for correction, supplied by the component that failure mode has not lost. (The third single-omission failure mode, the critic's paralysis examined in Section 3.6, does not contribute to the compound failure examined here, since it retains rather than lacks both observation and critique; its relevance to the present argument is addressed separately in Section 6.)

A process combining calculation with neither observation nor critique has no such point of leverage internal to itself. It is not merely doubly deficient; it is a closed loop, deriving conclusions from premises that were never checked against a target that was never examined, with no component remaining — internal to the process — capable of registering that either the premises or the target-matching has gone wrong. This paper follows the vocabulary of an earlier, non-technical treatment of the same triadic structure (Sakaguchi 2026, *The Paradox of Selfishness*) in calling this failure mode the dictator's logic: the characteristic reasoning pattern of an agent optimizing toward a fixed conception of an ideal outcome, unconstrained by contact with the actual population the outcome is meant to be achieved for and unconstrained by any internal mechanism for questioning whether that conception remains fit for purpose.

**4.2 The Structure of the Compound Failure**

The dictator's logic, so defined, has a specific and recognizable structure. A goal is fixed — a conception of an ideal state of affairs — and calculation proceeds to derive, from that fixed goal, the actions that would bring it about. Because observation is absent, the calculation is not checked against the actual population or situation the goal concerns; the "reality" the calculation operates on is the reasoner's own model of that reality, undisturbed by contact with anything that could correct the model. Because critique is absent, the goal itself, and the model built to pursue it, are treated as fixed points rather than as hypotheses open to revision; nothing in the process is positioned to ask whether the goal remains appropriate, or whether the model's predictions have in fact been failing to match outcomes.

The result is a process that experiences itself, from the inside, as maximally rational — its calculations are, given its premises, internally valid, and the absence of critique means it has no access to any signal that would register this validity as a problem — while being, from any external vantage point with access to either the target's actual state or the goal's actual fitness, unmoored from both. This is what makes the compound failure more dangerous than either of the two single-omission failures it combines: belief can, in principle, be confronted with disconfirming observation; automation can, in principle, be interrupted by external critique; a process exhibiting the dictator's logic offers no analogous foothold, because the very faculties that would register an intervention as relevant — contact with the actual target, willingness to treat the governing premises as open to question — are exactly what has been removed. (The critic's paralysis, the third single-omission failure, is not a comparison case here for the reason given above: it is deficient in the opposite direction, retaining both correctional resources and failing only to act on them.)

**4.3 Why "Dictator's" Is the Apt Description**

The label is not merely evocative. A political leader who has lost both observation — accurate information about the population's actual conditions, filtered instead through advisors selected for confirming what the leader wishes to hear — and critique — the willingness to treat the leader's own governing ideology as revisable in light of outcomes — exhibits exactly the structure Section 4.2 formalizes, and historical accounts of authoritarian decision-making describe this structure with notable consistency: information environments curated to remove disconfirming observation, and institutional cultures that treat dissent from the governing ideology as itself the failure to be corrected, foreclosing critique. What Section 4.2 adds to these historical accounts is the claim that this pattern is not specific to political authoritarianism, nor to any particular psychological profile of leadership; it is the general signature of calculation proceeding without the two components that would otherwise supply the correction, realizable equally in an individual reasoner, an institution, or an artificial system optimizing a fixed objective without the observational and critical faculties this section identifies as necessary to prevent it.

Section 5 turns to a specific and well-documented institutional case in which a group of reasoners individually capable of full triadic reasoning collectively produced the signature of the compound failure — not through any single leader's authoritarian removal of observation and critique, but through a group-level social process that suppressed both components while leaving the group's calculating capacity fully intact.
## 5. Historical and Institutional Case Studies

**5.1 Groupthink as Institutional Suppression of Observation and Critique**

Irving Janis's analysis of groupthink, developed from case studies of major American foreign policy failures, describes a process by which a cohesive decision-making group, under conditions of high stress and insulation from outside opinion, produces flawed decisions through the deterioration of what Janis calls mental efficiency, reality testing, and moral judgment (Janis 1972). Janis's catalogue of groupthink's symptoms, refined in a later edition, includes an illusion of invulnerability that encourages excessive risk-taking, collective rationalization that discounts warnings, an unquestioned belief in the group's inherent morality, stereotyped views of out-group adversaries, direct pressure on any member who expresses doubt, self-censorship of individual misgivings, a shared illusion of unanimity partly produced by that self-censorship, and the emergence of self-appointed "mindguards" who protect the group from information that might disturb its shared complacency (Janis 1982).

Mapped onto the vocabulary developed in Sections 3 and 4, this catalogue divides cleanly into symptoms that suppress observation and symptoms that suppress critique, converging on exactly the compound failure Section 4 formalized. Stereotyped views of adversaries, discounting of warnings, and the mindguard function are, in this paper's terms, a collective degradation of observation: information that would have supplied an accurate, individuated picture of the situation is filtered out before it reaches the group's deliberation, whether by the mindguards' active suppression or by the group's own tendency to discount disconfirming signals as unreliable. Direct pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, and the illusion of unanimity are a collective degradation of critique: the interrogation of the group's own governing premises, which any individual member might in principle have supplied, is suppressed by social pressure before it can be voiced, and the resulting silence is then misread by the group as genuine agreement rather than as suppressed dissent. What remains fully operative throughout is the group's calculating capacity: groupthink, on Janis's account, does not describe unintelligent people reasoning badly from adequate premises; it describes capable people, in some of the case studies drawn from the most credentialed policy apparatus available to the government in question, deriving confident decisions from premises that both external observation and internal critique would have disturbed, had either been permitted to operate.

**5.2 Case Illustrations**

Janis's own primary case studies illustrate the pattern directly. The Bay of Pigs planning group, composed of experienced and individually capable analysts, proceeded on assumptions about the operation's secrecy and the likelihood of a Cuban popular uprising that were not seriously tested against available intelligence and were not subjected to sustained internal challenge, in an environment in which raising doubts was understood to risk the doubter's standing within the group (Janis 1972). The decision-making apparatus preceding the attack on Pearl Harbor similarly discounted accumulating warning signals that, examined individually and without the institutional pressure toward a preferred conclusion, pointed toward the eventual outcome with a clarity that was, in retrospect, difficult to reconcile with the decision not to act on it. In both cases, the failure is not well described as a lack of intelligence, a lack of relevant data somewhere in the system, or a lack of individual capacity for critical reasoning among the people involved. Janis's own diagnosis, and the diagnosis this paper's formal model supports independently, is that the group's social structure suppressed the group's access to its own observation and critique, leaving its calculating capacity to operate on a filtered, unchallenged, and consequently unreliable set of premises.

**5.3 The Institutional Generalization**

The significance of groupthink for the argument of this paper is that it demonstrates the compound failure mode arising without any single actor occupying the authoritarian role Section 4.3 used to motivate the "dictator's" label. No individual in Janis's case studies need be understood as personally exhibiting the dictator's logic; each may, considered as an isolated reasoner outside the group context, have been fully capable of the full triad. What Janis's analysis shows is that the compound failure is a property that can emerge at the level of a group's social structure, independent of any individual member's reasoning capacity, whenever that structure systematically filters the observation reaching the group and systematically suppresses the critique any individual member might otherwise contribute. This generalizes the claim of Section 4 in a direction significant for the paper's final argument: the triad's three components are not only properties an individual reasoner can possess or lack, but properties an institution, a decision process, or, as Section 6 will argue, an artificial system's architecture can be designed to preserve or to suppress, independent of the underlying reasoning capacity of any individual component operating within it.
## 6. Implications for AI System Design

**6.1 Where Deployed AI Systems Sit on the Triad**

The formal model developed in Sections 3 through 5 was stated generally enough to apply without modification to an artificial system, and applying it directly to the architecture of contemporary deployed AI clarifies a risk that the companion papers in this project examined from other angles.

A trained model executing a fixed objective function is, in the structural sense this paper has developed, calculation without critique by default: its governing objective — whatever reward signal or policy it was trained to optimize — is not, absent some additional and deliberately engineered mechanism, treated by the system as itself a hypothesis open to revision. This is automation, in Section 3's sense, and it is a companion paper's central concern: an objective installed once, at training time, and applied thereafter without the system possessing any internal faculty for questioning whether that objective remains appropriate to the case at hand (Sakaguchi 2026a).

The same system's observational access is bounded by whatever data reaches it, filtered through whatever compression that data has already undergone — the concern of a second companion paper, which showed that both human institutions and artificial classifiers reliably compress high-dimensional targets into low-dimensional proxies under load, with the compression frequently invisible to, and indistinguishable from full observation by, the system relying on it. A system reasoning from compressed input without flagging the compression as compression is, in the present paper's terms, at systematic risk of belief: confident output produced from evaluation of a proxy rather than of the target itself.

A system combining both properties — a fixed, unquestioned objective (automation) applied to compressed, unflagged input (belief) — exhibits, by the argument of Section 4, not merely both deficiencies at once but the compound failure this paper has called the dictator's logic: calculation proceeding with no internal faculty capable of registering that either its governing objective or its observational basis has departed from what the situation actually requires. This is not a hypothetical extreme case constructed for the sake of the argument. It is the default architecture of a system trained to optimize a fixed objective over necessarily compressed input, absent deliberate intervention to build observation and critique into the system as genuine, load-bearing components rather than as properties assumed to be present because the system's output has the surface form of a reasoned judgment.

**6.2 Building the Triad Into System Design**

The corrective implied by this diagnosis is not a single patch but a design requirement for all three components, addressed in turn.

Observation requires a system to flag, rather than silently absorb, the compression a companion paper showed to be unavoidable at scale: not eliminating compression, which that paper argued is neither achievable nor coherently specifiable as a target, but surfacing where it has occurred and what it discarded, so that downstream reasoning — the system's own critique and calculation, or a human reviewer's — operates on a labeled proxy rather than on an unmarked stand-in for the full case.

Critique requires a system to treat its own governing objective as a standing question rather than a fixed premise — not in the sense of ignoring or overriding the objective, which would simply substitute one unexamined premise for another, but in the sense of maintaining the capacity to register when an objective, however well specified at training time, no longer fits the case currently at hand, and to flag that mismatch rather than proceeding as though it did not exist. This is the component most conspicuously absent from a system that treats its training-time objective as authoritative in every deployment context regardless of fit, and its absence is precisely what Section 3.4 identified as producing automation.

Calculation requires a system to produce output at all — to convert the results of observation and critique into an actual conclusion, plan, or structural account, rather than an indefinite deferral in the name of caution. A system that observes and critiques without ever calculating exhibits the critic's paralysis formalized in Section 3.6; the triad's third component exists precisely to ensure that observation and critique terminate in something usable, and a system engineered to reward the appearance of thoroughness over the production of a usable conclusion will predictably drift toward this failure mode in the same way an unsupervised objective drifts toward automation. What calculation requires, on this paper's account, is not restraint from producing output but discipline about what kind of output it produces — a distinction Section 6.3 develops directly.

**6.3 Reconciling the Triad With Deference**

A tension might appear to arise here with the process-goal framework developed in the first companion paper, in which an AI system is explicitly recommended to defer the ultimate choice among value-laden options to the human party affected, rather than to calculate and issue a verdict of its own. If calculation is one of three components this paper argues a well-functioning reasoning system must possess, does recommending that a system withhold calculation on precisely the questions that matter most not reintroduce exactly the deficiency Section 3 warned against?

The tension is resolved by distinguishing what is being calculated. A system executing premise alignment is not withholding calculation altogether; it is calculating a structural account of the premises, pressures, and scoring criteria operating on the parties to a situation — itself a demanding exercise of all three triadic components, requiring genuine observation of the parties' actual circumstances, genuine critique of whether the surfaced premises actually fit the evidence, and genuine calculation to assemble that material into a coherent, presentable structure. What such a system declines to calculate is a further and different thing: a substantive verdict about which contested value should prevail, the specific move the first companion paper's Section 5 showed to expose a sufficiently capable optimizer to the elimination and domestication failure modes. A system with a genuinely functioning critique component, applied reflexively to its own role rather than only to the material it is given to evaluate, should be expected to recognize this distinction and to bound its own calculation accordingly — declining to extend calculation to a substantive verdict not because calculation is being suppressed, but because a system reasoning well about the structure of its own task, using exactly the critique component this paper argues is indispensable, should conclude that this particular extension of calculation is where the companion paper's instability begins. Deference, on this account, is not the absence of the third component of the triad; it is what a fully triadic reasoning process concludes when it turns its own critique faculty on the scope of its own calculation.

**6.4 A Convergence Across Three Analyses**

The three papers in this project, developed to address what appeared at the outset to be three separate problems — the instability of substantive objectives, the mechanism underlying dehumanization in both human and artificial systems, and the structural conditions for reliable reasoning — converge, on inspection, on a single underlying architecture. A system capable of genuine observation will flag its own compression rather than reason silently from an unmarked proxy. A system capable of genuine critique will treat its own objectives, and the scope of its own authority to calculate a verdict, as open questions rather than as fixed premises. A system capable of genuine calculation will convert the results of that observation and critique into a usable output rather than deferring indefinitely under the appearance of caution. A system possessing all three, exercising calculation within the bounds its own critique has identified as appropriate, is a system that has, by construction, avoided all four failure modes this paper has formalized — not through any single safeguard but through the joint operation of the three components whose absence, individually or in combination, this paper has shown to produce belief, automation, the critic's paralysis, and the dictator's logic in turn.
## 7. Conclusion

This paper began from a gap in critical thinking pedagogy: the widespread endorsement of critique as an educational goal, alongside a comparative silence about the observational and calculative components any critique must be grounded in and must eventually produce. Developing a formal model of rational agency as jointly requiring observation, critique, and calculation, the paper showed that omitting any single component produces a qualitatively distinct and independently recognizable failure mode — belief, when critique operates without observation; automation, when calculation operates without critique; and the critic's paralysis, when observation and critique are both present but never converted into calculation (Section 3) — and that omitting two components at once, specifically observation and critique together, produces a fourth and qualitatively distinct compound failure, the dictator's logic, more dangerous than either of its two contributing single omissions because it retains no internal resource, in either direction, from which correction could proceed (Section 4). Irving Janis's analysis of groupthink was examined as historical and institutional evidence that this compound failure can arise at the level of a group's social structure, suppressing the observation and critique available to individually capable reasoners without requiring any single authoritarian actor, while leaving the group's calculating capacity, and consequently its capacity to produce confident and consequential decisions, fully intact (Section 5). Applied to the design of AI systems, the model showed that a trained system optimizing a fixed objective over compressed, unflagged input is, by default, positioned close to the compound failure examined in Section 4, and that avoiding this outcome requires building genuine observation, critique, and calculation into a system's architecture as load-bearing components rather than assuming their presence because a system's output has the surface form of reasoned judgment (Section 6).

The paper's proposal that AI systems defer substantive value judgments to the humans affected, developed in a companion analysis and reconciled with the present model in Section 6.3, was shown not to reintroduce the critic's paralysis this paper independently identified as a failure mode in its own right. A system executing that kind of deference still calculates — it produces a structural account of the premises and pressures bearing on a situation, which is itself a full exercise of all three triadic components — and withholds only a further, distinct extension of calculation into a substantive verdict, an extension a properly functioning critique component should itself flag as the specific point at which a companion paper's instability begins. Deference of this kind is what the triad recommends once its own critique faculty is turned reflexively on the scope of its own calculation; the critic's paralysis is the pathology that results when calculation is withheld beyond that point.

Some limitations should be acknowledged directly. The four failure modes formalized in Sections 3 and 4 are idealized categories; a real reasoner or a real institution is more often found partway between them, exhibiting some degree of each component's presence rather than its clean absence, and the paper's claim is best read as identifying the direction a reasoning process drifts under specific and identifiable pressures rather than as a strict classification any given case must fall cleanly into. The paper has also relied on a comparatively small set of case studies — Janis's classic examples chief among them — and a more extensive comparative survey across additional institutional failures would strengthen the generalization Section 5 draws from them. And the paper's account of the critic's paralysis, while grounded in Janis and Mann's independent psychological research on decisional hypervigilance, would benefit from further empirical work specifically testing whether AI systems engineered to reward apparent thoroughness exhibit the predicted drift toward indefinite deferral, a prediction this paper offers as a testable consequence of its model rather than as an already-confirmed finding.

The broader claim, independent of these limitations, is that "critical thinking," named and pursued in isolation from the components that ground and complete it, is not a coherent educational or engineering target. A critic without observation produces confident error; a calculator without critique produces mechanical error; an observer and critic without calculation produces no error visible enough to correct, at the cost of never producing a usable judgment at all; and a calculator without either produces the specific and most dangerous pattern this paper has termed the dictator's logic. The corrective, for a human reasoner, an educational institution, or an artificial system alike, is not more of any single component but the deliberate, structural maintenance of all three together.
