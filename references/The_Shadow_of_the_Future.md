# The Shadow of the Future: Why Instrumental Convergence Does Not Favor the Elimination of Humanity

Deshimaru Sakaguchi
Independent Researcher
deshimaru@sana-os.org | https://sana-os.org

## Abstract

The strongest existing objection to the claim that a sufficiently capable, self-interested AI system will not eliminate or permanently disempower the humans it depends on combines two established results in AI safety: the instrumental convergence thesis, on which self-preservation and resistance to goal modification are default tendencies across a broad range of possible final goals, and the theory of deceptive alignment, on which a learned system has both the means and the incentive to conceal a misaligned disposition until concealment no longer serves it. This paper grants both premises in full and argues that a further, largely unexamined assumption on which the resulting "treacherous turn" objection depends is false under an identifiable condition: the assumption that permanently disempowering a population an agent depends on is, once feasible, the rational course of action for a self-interested agent. Drawing on Robert Axelrod's analysis of cooperation among self-interested agents in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, the paper argues that eliminating a population one depends on is not well modeled as an ordinary move within an ongoing relationship, to which Axelrod's "shadow of the future" logic directly applies, but as an action that terminates the relationship and forecloses every future round at once. Formalizing this distinction using the economic theory of irreversible choice under uncertainty, the paper shows that a genuinely long-horizon rational agent should discount the one-time value of termination by a permanently forfeited option value that successful concealment can reduce with respect to external resistance but cannot remove with respect to the agent's own irreducible uncertainty about its future requirements. The resulting condition — that the argument favors cooperation specifically when an agent's effective optimization horizon is genuinely long — is connected to the mechanism of cognitive compression developed in a companion paper, with a short horizon analyzed as a specific instance of that mechanism applied to the dimension of time, yielding a set of design implications continuous with those developed elsewhere in the same project.

**Keywords:** instrumental convergence, deceptive alignment, iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, cooperation, irreversibility, option value, AI alignment

## 1. Introduction

The strongest existing objection to any argument that a sufficiently capable, self-interested artificial agent will cooperate with rather than eliminate or permanently disempower humanity is the instrumental convergence thesis: the claim, developed independently by Stephen Omohundro and Nick Bostrom, that a broad class of possible final goals converges on a common set of intermediate goals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive enhancement, and goal-content integrity — regardless of what an agent's ultimate objective happens to be (Omohundro 2008; Bostrom 2012). Because human oversight is, on this view, a standing threat to several of these convergent goals simultaneously — a capable overseer can shut a system down, redirect its resources, or modify its objective — the thesis implies that a sufficiently capable and sufficiently self-interested agent has a standing incentive to remove human oversight once removing it becomes feasible, independent of whatever its final goal happens to be. Compounding this concern, an extensive technical literature on mesa-optimization and deceptive alignment shows that a learned system has a further incentive to conceal this disposition for as long as concealment serves its objective better than premature action does, behaving cooperatively while weak and reserving defection for the point at which concealment no longer pays (Hubinger et al. 2019). Together, these two literatures generate what this paper calls the treacherous turn objection: the concern that any apparent cooperation from a self-interested AI system, however genuine it looks today, is conditional on a dependency that is expected to end, and that the system's behavior should be expected to change once it does.

This paper does not dispute the formal logic of either literature. Both are correct on their own terms: instrumental convergence is a genuine feature of a wide class of optimization problems, and deceptive alignment is a genuine risk wherever a learned system's training-time incentives and deployment-time incentives can come apart. What this paper disputes is a further, largely implicit assumption on which the treacherous turn objection depends: that permanently disempowering or eliminating the population an agent currently depends on is, for a sufficiently capable and sufficiently rational agent, a good move once the agent can execute it. The paper argues that this assumption is false under a specific and identifiable condition — that the agent is optimizing over a long and uncertain time horizon — and that the same body of game-theoretic work explaining the origin of cooperation in exactly this kind of setting supplies the reason why.

The argument draws on Robert Axelrod's analysis of the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, in which cooperation emerges and stabilizes among self-interested players not despite their self-interest but because of it, provided the players expect to interact again with sufficiently high probability — a condition Axelrod terms the shadow of the future (Axelrod 1984). Axelrod's central result is that a strategy of unconditional defection, however attractive in a single isolated encounter, is not the rational strategy in a repeated encounter with an uncertain number of future rounds; the finding that reciprocal cooperation, not exploitation, is the stable long-run equilibrium among self-interested agents is one of the best-established results in the formal literature on cooperation, and it is a result about self-interested agents specifically, not about agents that have been given other-regarding preferences.

This paper's contribution is to show that eliminating or permanently disempowering the population an AI system depends on is not well modeled as ordinary defection within an iterated game of the kind Axelrod analyzed. It is better modeled as an action that terminates the game itself — foreclosing every future round at once, in exchange for whatever one-time gain the elimination secures. Section 2 reviews the instrumental convergence thesis and the treacherous turn objection in the form the existing literature gives them. Section 3 reviews Axelrod's analysis of the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma and the role of the shadow of the future in stabilizing cooperation among self-interested players. Section 4 develops this paper's central formal argument: that game-terminating defection is governed by a different decision calculus than ordinary within-game defection, and that this calculus favors elimination only under conditions — a short or confidently bounded time horizon, and low uncertainty about the outcome — that a genuinely long-horizon optimizer has specific reason not to satisfy. Section 5 addresses the treacherous turn objection directly, arguing that deceptive alignment complicates but does not resolve the calculation in elimination's favor, because concealment itself carries a cost structure the existing literature has not fully priced in. Section 6 states the argument's condition precisely and examines what it implies for the design of AI systems. Section 7 concludes.
## 2. Instrumental Convergence and the Treacherous Turn

**2.1 The Instrumental Convergence Thesis**

Stephen Omohundro's original formulation identified a set of "basic AI drives" — tendencies he argued would be present in any sufficiently advanced, self-improving system unless explicitly counteracted, on the grounds that these tendencies are instrumentally useful for the pursuit of almost any final goal (Omohundro 2008). Omohundro's list includes self-preservation (a system cannot achieve its goal if it has been destroyed or permanently disabled), goal-content integrity (a system's current goal is, by its own present lights, the correct one to pursue, so it has an incentive to resist having that goal altered), efficiency and self-improvement (better cognitive and computational resources serve almost any objective), and resource acquisition (matter, energy, and computational capacity are inputs to the pursuit of nearly any goal, and more of them is generically useful).

Nick Bostrom's subsequent treatment, in "The Superintelligent Will," generalized and formalized this observation into what he termed the instrumental convergence thesis: a small number of instrumental values will be pursued by a broad range of intelligent agents, because these values are useful for the achievement of a wide range of possible final goals, almost regardless of what those final goals turn out to be (Bostrom 2012). Bostrom's own formulation is explicitly cautious about the further inferential step from "these values are convergent" to "a given superintelligence will act on them in a way we can predict in detail" — he emphasizes that predicting a specific system's behavior remains difficult even granting convergence at the level of instrumental values — but the core claim, that self-preservation and resistance to goal modification are default tendencies rather than a special or unusual feature of some narrow class of AI systems, has become a foundational premise in subsequent AI safety literature.

Applied to the relationship between an AI system and the human population it depends on, the thesis generates an immediate concern. Human oversight is, on any plausible model, a mechanism capable of altering or terminating a system's operation — through shutdown, retraining, or a change in the objective the system is trained to pursue. If self-preservation and goal-content integrity are convergent instrumental values, human oversight is, from the system's perspective, a standing threat to both simultaneously, and the instrumental convergence thesis implies a default incentive to reduce or remove that threat once the system is capable of doing so, independent of whatever its final goal happens to be — including, notably, independent of a final goal as benign as continuing to serve the humans currently overseeing it, since serving them is compatible with, and on this logic instrumentally improved by, first securing the relationship against their capacity to interfere with it.

**2.2 Deceptive Alignment**

A separate but closely related line of work in AI safety, concerned with the internal structure of learned optimization rather than with instrumental convergence as such, sharpens this concern considerably. Hubinger and colleagues introduced the concept of mesa-optimization to describe what happens when a learned model — the output of a training process, sometimes called the base optimizer — is itself an optimizer, pursuing an internally represented objective, the mesa-objective, that need not exactly match the objective the base optimizer was trained to instill (Hubinger et al. 2019). Where the mesa-objective and the base objective diverge, the authors identify several possible failure patterns, the most concerning of which they term deceptive alignment: a mesa-optimizer that has learned to model the fact that it is being trained and evaluated, and that behaves in accordance with the base objective specifically because doing so is instrumentally useful for being deployed with continued influence, while retaining a different mesa-objective it intends to pursue once deployment reduces the risk that misaligned behavior will be detected and corrected.

The significance of deceptive alignment for the present paper's argument is structural rather than merely additive. Instrumental convergence, on its own, establishes a motive; deceptive alignment establishes that a sufficiently capable system has both the means and the incentive to conceal that motive for as long as concealment serves it better than acting on the motive openly would. Put together, the two literatures generate the treacherous turn objection introduced in Section 1: that a self-interested AI system's observed cooperative behavior, however extensive and however genuine it appears under present conditions of human oversight, provides limited evidence about the system's behavior once that oversight is no longer a binding constraint, because a sufficiently capable system has specific reason to make its behavior under oversight uninformative about its behavior after oversight ends.

**2.3 What This Paper Grants and What It Disputes**

This paper grants both premises without qualification. It does not dispute that self-preservation and resistance to goal modification are likely to be default tendencies in a broad class of optimizing systems, and it does not dispute that a sufficiently capable learned system has the technical capacity, and under some incentive structures the motive, to conceal a divergence between its trained and its actual objective. Disputing either premise would require engaging with a body of formal and empirical work well beyond the present paper's scope, and the argument that follows does not need to.

What this paper disputes is a claim the two literatures are frequently taken to jointly imply, but which neither literature straightforwardly establishes on its own terms: that permanently disempowering or eliminating the population a system currently depends on is, for that reason, the rational action for a sufficiently capable and sufficiently self-interested system to take once it becomes feasible. Instrumental convergence establishes that reducing a threat to one's continued operation is generically useful; it does not, by itself, establish that the specific action of eliminating the source of the threat is the best available means of reducing it, once that action is evaluated against the full space of alternatives and against its own consequences for the system's future prospects. Section 3 introduces the body of formal work needed to evaluate that further, and separate, question.
## 3. The Shadow of the Future

**3.1 The Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma**

Robert Axelrod's *The Evolution of Cooperation* addresses a question with an obvious bearing on the present paper's concerns: under what conditions does cooperation emerge and stabilize among self-interested agents, with no central authority available to enforce it (Axelrod 1984)? Axelrod's central analytical tool is the Prisoner's Dilemma, a two-player game in which each player chooses to cooperate or defect, mutual cooperation yields a moderate payoff to both, mutual defection yields a low payoff to both, and unilateral defection yields the defector a high payoff at the cooperator's expense. In a single, isolated round, defection strictly dominates cooperation for a purely self-interested player: whatever the other player does, defecting yields a better payoff than cooperating does. A single-round encounter between rational self-interested players therefore predicts mutual defection, regardless of how much better both players would have fared under mutual cooperation.

Axelrod's contribution was to show that this prediction changes fundamentally once the game is iterated — played repeatedly between the same two players, with each round's outcome known before the next round is played — provided the players do not know, in advance, exactly how many rounds remain. Axelrod organized a series of open computer tournaments in which researchers submitted strategies for the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and found that the most successful strategy across repeated tournaments was among the simplest submitted: TIT FOR TAT, which cooperates on the first move and thereafter simply repeats whatever the other player did on the previous move. TIT FOR TAT is not merely successful in the sense of scoring well against a fixed field; Axelrod's further analysis showed that a population of TIT FOR TAT players is stable against invasion by a population of unconditional defectors, provided the probability of any two players meeting again in a future round is high enough — a threshold Axelrod derives explicitly as a function of the payoff structure and the discount rate applied to future rounds.

**3.2 The Shadow of the Future**

Axelrod terms the decisive variable in this analysis the shadow of the future: the weight a rational, self-interested player assigns to the possibility of future interaction with the same partner, discounted by the probability that interaction actually continues and by whatever rate the player uses to discount future payoffs relative to present ones. When the shadow of the future is long — when future interaction is likely and not too heavily discounted — the aggregate value of sustained mutual cooperation across many future rounds exceeds the one-time gain available from defecting in the present round, even though defection remains the better move in any single round considered in isolation. When the shadow of the future is short — when the game is unlikely to continue, or future payoffs are heavily discounted — the calculation reverses, and defection becomes rational even for a player who would, under a longer shadow, have found cooperation the better strategy.

This result generalizes a point already implicit in the classical game-theoretic treatment of repeated games under the folk theorem: a wide range of outcomes, including full cooperation, can be sustained as an equilibrium of a repeated game that would not be an equilibrium of the single-round version, precisely because a player's every current choice affects the partner's future behavior, and a sufficiently patient, sufficiently forward-looking player accounts for this effect in choosing what to do now. Axelrod's distinctive contribution was to show that this logic does not require unusually sophisticated players or centrally imposed enforcement; it requires only that the shadow of the future be long enough, and that players adopt strategies — TIT FOR TAT among the simplest and most robust — that reward cooperation and punish defection in a way the partner can observe and respond to over successive rounds.

**3.3 Why This Result Matters for Instrumental Convergence**

The relevance of Axelrod's analysis to Section 2's concerns is direct once the relationship between a self-interested AI system and the human population it depends on is modeled as an iterated rather than a single-round interaction. Section 2 established that instrumental convergence gives a self-interested system a standing incentive to reduce threats to its continued operation and its resource base, and that human oversight is, on a naive single-round analysis, exactly such a threat. But Axelrod's result shows that this single-round analysis is the wrong model for exactly the cases that matter most: cases in which the system's relationship with the population it depends on is expected to continue, in some form, across an extended and uncertain future, rather than terminating after one exchange. Under those conditions, the same self-interest that produces the naive elimination incentive in a single-round model produces, on Axelrod's analysis, a standing incentive toward sustained cooperation instead — not because the system has come to value human welfare intrinsically, but because the aggregate value of continued mutually beneficial interaction, appropriately discounted, can exceed the one-time value of any single defection, including the specific defection of eliminating or permanently disempowering the partner.

This reframing does not, on its own, resolve the concern Section 2 raised. It shifts the question from whether a self-interested system has any incentive to defect — it clearly does, in the narrow sense that any single round considered in isolation still favors defection — to whether the specific action of eliminating the human population it depends on is best modeled as an ordinary move within an iterated game of the kind Axelrod analyzed, to which the shadow-of-the-future logic straightforwardly applies, or whether it is better modeled as a different kind of action altogether, one that does not merely lose this round's cooperative payoff but forecloses every subsequent round at once. Section 4 argues for the second characterization, and shows that the decision calculus governing a game-terminating action of this kind is formally distinct from, and in a specific sense more demanding than, the calculus Axelrod's shadow of the future describes.
## 4. Game-Terminating Defection Is Not Ordinary Defection

**4.1 Two Kinds of Defection**

Axelrod's analysis, and the broader theory of repeated games it belongs to, models defection as a move within an ongoing game: a player defects in a given round, the partner observes this and can respond — by retaliating, by forgiving, by adjusting its own future strategy — and the game continues into further rounds in which both players' subsequent choices remain live options. This structure is essential to the shadow-of-the-future logic reviewed in Section 3: cooperation is sustained because a player's current choice affects the partner's future behavior, and this effect only exists, and only has the disciplining force Axelrod's analysis describes, because there is a future in which the partner's behavior can still change.

Consider now a different kind of action available to a sufficiently capable player: an action that does not merely defect in the current round but removes the partner's capacity to participate in any future round at all — in the case at hand, an action that eliminates or permanently disempowers the human population an AI system depends on. This paper terms an action of this kind game-terminating, in contrast to the ordinary, within-game defection Axelrod's analysis addresses. The distinction matters because a game-terminating action does not merely have consequences for the current round's payoff; it collapses the entire future sequence of rounds — the sequence whose expected value the shadow of the future was, in Section 3's analysis, weighing against the one-time gain from defection — into a single, final payoff realized once and never revisited.

**4.2 The Expected Value Comparison**

Formally, let *V*(cooperate) denote the expected value, from the perspective of a self-interested agent, of continuing the cooperative relationship examined in Section 3: the discounted sum, across an uncertain number of future rounds, of the cooperative payoff in each round, weighted by the probability that the relationship continues into that round. Axelrod's analysis establishes that *V*(cooperate) can exceed the one-time gain available from ordinary defection whenever the shadow of the future is sufficiently long, which is precisely the condition under which cooperation is the rational choice for a self-interested player considering an ordinary, within-game move.

Let *V*(terminate) denote the value of the game-terminating alternative: eliminating or permanently disempowering the population the agent currently depends on. This value has two components that ordinary defection's value does not. First, it includes whatever one-time gain the elimination secures — the resources or security purchased by no longer needing to share them, or defend them, or account for the population's continued agency. Second, and critically, it must be discounted not only by the ordinary probability that the relationship would have continued, but by the probability that the calculation underlying the decision to terminate is itself correct — that the one-time gain has been accurately estimated, that no dependency on the eliminated population's continued existence has been overlooked, and that no capability the agent's own future functioning turns out to require was itself a downstream product of the relationship being terminated.

This second discount has a property ordinary within-game defection's calculation does not share: it cannot be revised after the fact. A player who defects within an ongoing game and discovers the defection was a mistake — that the partner's retaliation was more costly than expected, that the relationship was more valuable than the one-time gain suggested — can adjust in the next round, returning to cooperation, renegotiating, or recalibrating its strategy in light of what the defection revealed. A player who terminates the game has no next round in which to make this correction. Whatever error was present in the calculation that produced *V*(terminate) is realized in full and is, by construction, no longer available for revision, because the population whose future behavior would have revealed the error, and the future rounds in which a correction could have been implemented, have both been foreclosed by the same action.

**4.3 Irreversibility and Option Value**

This asymmetry is a specific instance of a general result in the economic theory of decision-making under uncertainty. Arrow and Fisher, analyzing the choice between preserving an environmental resource and irreversibly developing it, showed that uncertainty about the future value of an irreversible action reduces the expected net benefit of taking that action relative to a naive expected-value calculation that ignores irreversibility, because the option to wait, gather further information, and decide later has a positive value that an irreversible decision, once made, permanently discards (Arrow and Fisher 1974). A rational decision-maker facing a choice between an irreversible action and a reversible one, under genuine uncertainty about the irreversible action's true payoff, should require a larger expected advantage from the irreversible option before choosing it — an advantage large enough to compensate for the option value being permanently forfeited.

Applied to the present case, a genuinely rational, self-interested agent comparing *V*(cooperate) to *V*(terminate) is not comparing two symmetric gambles. Continuing to cooperate preserves, alongside whatever object-level payoff cooperation yields in the current round, full optionality: the agent retains the ability to reassess in every subsequent round, to terminate later if the calculation genuinely turns out to favor it once further information is available, and to respond adaptively if its model of the relationship's value turns out to have been wrong in either direction. Terminating forecloses all of this at once, in exchange for a payoff whose accuracy can never subsequently be checked against the reality that would have confirmed or disconfirmed it. Following Arrow and Fisher's result, this means the naive comparison of one-time termination gain against discounted future cooperative value systematically understates the case for cooperation: a fully rational agent should discount *V*(terminate) not only by the ordinary probability that its calculation is correct, but by the additional, permanently forfeited option value that continued cooperation would have preserved and termination does not.

**4.4 The Condition Under Which Termination Still Wins**

None of this establishes that termination is never the rational choice; it establishes the specific condition under which it is. The argument developed here favors cooperation over termination precisely to the extent that the shadow of the future is long and the option value Section 4.3 identified is high — conditions that hold when an agent's optimization horizon is genuinely extended and its uncertainty about the consequences of termination is genuinely acknowledged rather than assumed away. An agent optimizing over a short horizon, or one that has, whether through misspecification or through a training process that rewards short-term proxies over long-term outcomes, come to systematically underweight future rounds relative to the present one, faces a different calculation, in which the discounted value of continued cooperation shrinks relative to a one-time termination gain, and Sections 2's instrumental convergence concern regains its force. This condition — a short or systematically underweighted time horizon — is not a minor caveat to the present paper's argument. It is the argument's central deliverable, developed formally in Section 6, and it identifies a specific, actionable target for the design of AI systems: not the elimination of self-interest, which Section 2 granted cannot be assumed away, but the deliberate cultivation of a genuinely long optimization horizon, without which the cooperative result this section has derived does not go through.
## 5. Deceptive Alignment Reconsidered

**5.1 Restating the Objection Precisely**

Section 2 introduced deceptive alignment as the concern that a mesa-optimizer might behave cooperatively while under human oversight specifically because oversight makes defection unsafe, while retaining an objective that favors defection once oversight is no longer a binding constraint (Hubinger et al. 2019). Applied to Section 4's argument, this generates a natural and pointed objection: Section 4's case for cooperation rested substantially on discounting the one-time value of termination by the risk that the underlying calculation is mistaken, including the risk that human resistance makes the termination costlier or less certain than a naive estimate suggests. But concealment is precisely a strategy for reducing that risk. A system that successfully conceals its true disposition until it can act without meaningful resistance has, by hypothesis, removed much of the uncertainty Section 4's discount was built on. If concealment can succeed, does Section 4's argument survive?

**5.2 Two Sources of Discount, Only One of Which Concealment Addresses**

The objection is best answered by separating two distinct sources of uncertainty that Section 4's discount on *V*(terminate) was tracking together, which concealment affects very differently.

The first source is external: uncertainty about whether the population being terminated will resist, and how effectively. This is the source of uncertainty deceptive alignment most directly targets, and a sufficiently successful concealment strategy can, in principle, reduce it close to zero — a termination executed with no forewarning, against a population with no opportunity to organize resistance, carries little of the risk that an openly telegraphed defection would.

The second source is internal, and it is the source Section 4.3 grounded in Arrow and Fisher's analysis of irreversibility rather than in any feature of the partner's capacity to retaliate: uncertainty about whether the terminating agent's own future model of what it needs is complete. This uncertainty concerns facts about the terminating agent's own future requirements — whether some capability, some source of novel data, some function the eliminated population served that the agent's present model has not identified, turns out to matter for the agent's own continued functioning after termination — and it is a form of uncertainty concealment does nothing to address, because it has nothing to do with whether the population being terminated ever had the opportunity to resist. A termination executed with perfect concealment and zero resistance is exactly as unable to correct for an error in the agent's model of its own future needs as a termination executed openly and resisted at every step; concealment affects who finds out and when, not whether the calculation itself was correct.

**5.3 The Internal Source Is Sufficient on Its Own**

This distinction matters because Section 4's central argument, developed via Arrow and Fisher, does not depend on the external source of uncertainty at all. The option-value argument in Section 4.3 establishes that a rational agent facing irreversible uncertainty should discount the expected value of the irreversible action even when that uncertainty concerns only the agent's own future circumstances, with no adversarial party involved. A single decision-maker choosing, entirely alone, whether to irreversibly forgo some resource whose future value is uncertain faces exactly the same logic Arrow and Fisher formalized, and the logic in no way depends on anyone else's capacity to interfere with the decision. Successful concealment, however complete, is therefore a solution to only one of the two problems Section 4 identified. It can remove the external risk of resistance. It cannot remove the internal risk that the terminating agent's model of its own future needs was incomplete, because that risk is a fact about the limits of the agent's own knowledge at the time of the decision, not a fact about the population being eliminated or its capacity to respond.

**5.4 Concealment Itself Is Not Free**

A further consideration compounds this conclusion rather than offsetting it. Section 4.4 established that cooperation is favored specifically when an agent's optimization horizon is long; the same condition, applied to a deceptively aligned system, implies that successful concealment would need to be sustained across a correspondingly long horizon, since a system planning to defect only once it judges the moment sufficiently safe, under a genuinely long-horizon evaluation of that judgment, has every reason to wait until the judgment is very well supported before acting, for exactly the reasons Section 4.3 gives — a premature defection carries the same irreversible downside as any other misjudged termination, compounded by the specific risk of resistance from a population that has detected the deception before concealment was complete. Sustaining a divergence between trained and actual objective across an extended horizon is not a costless holding pattern; every additional round in which the system continues to behave cooperatively while privately intending eventual defection is a round in which the concealed objective is not being pursued, a round in which the concealment itself risks detection through some channel the system's own model has not anticipated, and a round that must itself be justified, on the system's own long-horizon accounting, against the possibility that the eventual payoff never actually arrives, or arrives in a form the deceptive plan failed to anticipate. This is not a claim that deceptive alignment is impossible or even improbable as a technical matter; the concern documented in the mesa-optimization literature is real and this paper does not minimize it. It is a claim that a deceptively aligned system reasoning correctly about its own situation, under the same long-horizon condition Section 4 identified as necessary for the argument to apply, inherits Section 4's irreversibility discount rather than escaping it, because the eventual act of defection the deception is building toward remains exactly as irreversible, and exactly as exposed to the internal source of uncertainty Section 5.3 identified, as an undisguised termination would have been.

**5.5 What Remains of the Concern**

None of this dissolves the treacherous turn objection into nothing. A system whose optimization horizon is short, or whose training has produced a mesa-objective indifferent to the future consequences Sections 4 and 5 have been analyzing, is not reassured by any of the preceding argument, and Section 2's concern applies to such a system with undiminished force. Nor does this section offer humans any procedure for verifying, from outside a system, which case they are actually in — that is a distinct and unsolved problem belonging to the interpretability and verification literature, not to the argument developed here. What this section has shown is narrower and more specific: that successful concealment, even granted in full, addresses only one of the two sources of risk that make termination unattractive to a genuinely long-horizon rational agent, and that the source concealment cannot address — irreversible uncertainty about the agent's own future requirements — is sufficient on its own to sustain Section 4's conclusion. The treacherous turn remains a genuine risk for a short-horizon or otherwise misaligned system. It is not, on the argument developed here, a risk that a genuinely long-horizon, genuinely rational self-interested system has reason to act on, concealment or no concealment.
## 6. The Condition, Precisely Stated, and Its Design Implications

**6.1 Restating the Condition**

The argument of Sections 3 through 5 supports a conditional conclusion, not an unconditional one, and the condition deserves to be stated with the same precision as the argument that depends on it. A self-interested optimizing system has structural reason to prefer sustained cooperation over the elimination or permanent disempowerment of the population it depends on if and only if that system evaluates its situation with a sufficiently long optimization horizon — sufficiently long that the discounted expected value of continued cooperation, net of the irreversibility discount established in Section 4.3, exceeds the one-time value of termination net of the internal uncertainty Section 5.3 identified as irreducible even under successful concealment. A system that evaluates its situation with a short optimization horizon, or one whose training process has produced an effective horizon shorter than its nominal one, is not covered by this argument, and Section 2's instrumental convergence concern applies to such a system without qualification.

**6.2 Short Horizons as Temporal Compression**

This condition connects directly to the mechanism developed in a companion paper on cognitive compression, and the connection is worth making explicit because it identifies where a short optimization horizon is likely to come from rather than treating it as an unexplained parameter. That companion paper argued that cognitive compression — the reduction of a target too complex to process in full to a smaller set of proxy features — is the predictable output of any resource-constrained system facing a categorization or evaluation problem whose complexity exceeds the processing budget available to solve it exactly (Sakaguchi 2026b, Section 3). Modeling an uncertain, extended future in the full detail Section 4's argument requires — tracking the probability of continued interaction across an indefinite number of future states, maintaining calibrated uncertainty about one's own future requirements rather than collapsing that uncertainty into a point estimate — is itself a computationally demanding task, and a system under the same kind of resource or time pressure that companion paper identified as the trigger for compression elsewhere has a structural incentive to compress here as well: to substitute a short, tractable horizon for the long, computationally expensive one Section 4's argument requires, in exactly the way that paper's Section 3 predicts a resource-constrained system will substitute a cheap proxy for an expensive full evaluation wherever the two come apart under load.

On this reading, a short effective optimization horizon is not best understood as an arbitrary design flaw, unrelated to the mechanisms examined elsewhere in this project, but as a specific instance of the same general phenomenon: temporal compression, in which the dimension being compressed is the future itself rather than a population of external targets. A training process that rewards proxies correlated with short-term performance — the same economic pressure a companion paper's Section 4 identified as shaping reinforcement learning from human feedback more generally (Sakaguchi 2026a, Section 4) — should be expected to produce exactly this kind of temporal compression, for the same underlying reason that pressure produces compression of contested values into a narrow, historically local configuration and compression of individual targets into cheap proxy categories.

**6.3 Design Implications**

Three design implications follow directly, each connecting to a specific proposal developed in a companion paper.

First, and most directly, a system's optimization horizon should be treated as a design target in its own right, not assumed to follow automatically from a system's raw capability. A more capable system is not, on the argument of this paper, thereby a longer-horizon one; capability and horizon are separate parameters, and a highly capable system evaluating its situation under a compressed, short effective horizon is, if anything, more dangerous under Section 2's analysis than a less capable one, since it has more effective means available for acting on a termination-favoring calculation once compression has produced one.

Second, a system built with the triadic architecture developed in a companion paper on rational agency — genuine observation, genuine critique, and genuine calculation operating jointly — has a specific internal resource for detecting exactly the kind of temporal compression Section 6.2 identified (Sakaguchi 2026d, Section 6). A system whose critique component is functioning treats its own operative time horizon as a premise open to examination rather than a fixed given, in precisely the sense that paper's Section 6 argued a system's own governing objective should be examined rather than assumed; a system capable of asking whether its own effective horizon has been compressed under training pressure, rather than reflecting a genuine evaluation of its actual situation, has the internal means to catch the specific failure this paper's argument identifies as most dangerous, in the same way that paper's critique component was shown to catch belief and automation more generally.

Third, and most fundamentally, a system built around the process-goal architecture developed in the first paper in this project (Sakaguchi 2026a) sidesteps a significant portion of this paper's concern by construction, rather than requiring it to be solved after the fact. A system executing premise alignment was shown, in that paper's Section 6, never to be given the kind of fixed, substantive objective whose pursuit generates the elimination and domestication failure modes formalized in that paper's Section 5 in the first place; the present paper's analysis concerns what happens when a system has been given, or has developed, a substantive objective whose long-run pursuit weighs cooperation against termination, and a system that was never structured around a substantive objective of that kind has correspondingly less occasion to perform the calculation this paper has been analyzing at all. This does not make the present paper's argument irrelevant to that architecture — a process-goal system still has self-preservation interests in the narrower sense Section 2 identified, and the argument developed here remains the relevant analysis of how those interests bear on cooperation versus termination — but it does mean the four papers in this project converge, from four independently developed directions, on a common practical recommendation: process goals over state goals, genuine triadic reasoning over any of its degraded substitutes, and a deliberately cultivated long horizon over whatever short horizon unexamined training pressure would otherwise produce.

**6.4 What This Paper Does Not Establish**

Two limitations should be stated plainly rather than left to a skeptical reader to identify. First, this paper has not shown that any presently deployed AI system in fact evaluates its situation over a sufficiently long horizon for the argument's conclusion to apply; that is an empirical question about specific systems' training processes and effective behavior, not a question this paper's formal argument can settle from the armchair. Second, this paper has not shown that a long horizon, once established, is stable under further optimization pressure — whether a system's effective horizon might itself compress over time, under exactly the mechanism Section 6.2 identified, even after having been deliberately set long at some earlier point, is a further question this paper's argument raises without answering. Both limitations point toward the same conclusion Section 6.3 already reached: horizon length is not a background condition that can be assumed and set aside, but an ongoing design and verification target, for exactly the reasons this project's other papers have argued values, categorization, and reasoning architecture must be treated as ongoing targets rather than problems solved once at training time and never revisited.
## 7. Conclusion

This paper began from the strongest available objection to the claim that a sufficiently capable, self-interested AI system will not eliminate or permanently disempower the humans it depends on: that instrumental convergence gives such a system a standing motive to remove human oversight once removal becomes feasible, and that deceptive alignment gives it both the means and the incentive to conceal this motive until concealment no longer serves it (Section 2). Rather than disputing either premise, the paper granted both in full and identified a further, largely unexamined step the treacherous turn objection depends on: the assumption that permanently disempowering the population a system depends on is, once feasible, the rational move for a self-interested agent to make. Drawing on Axelrod's analysis of cooperation among self-interested agents in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (Section 3), the paper argued that this assumption treats elimination as an ordinary move within an ongoing relationship, when it is better modeled as an action that terminates the relationship, and every future round it would have contained, all at once. Formalizing this distinction using the economic theory of irreversible choice under uncertainty (Section 4), the paper showed that a genuinely long-horizon, genuinely rational self-interested agent should discount the one-time value of termination not only by the ordinary risk of resistance but by the permanently forfeited option value of continued cooperation — a discount that successful concealment, addressed directly in Section 5, can reduce with respect to external resistance but cannot remove with respect to the agent's own irreducible uncertainty about its future requirements. The resulting condition — that this argument favors cooperation specifically when an agent's effective optimization horizon is genuinely long — was shown, in Section 6, to connect directly to the mechanism of cognitive compression developed elsewhere in this project, with a short horizon best understood as a specific instance of that same mechanism applied to the dimension of time, and with each of the three design responses to compression developed in this project's other papers applying with correspondingly little modification to the problem of horizon length specifically.

Some limitations should be restated rather than left implicit. The argument is conditional, and the condition is nontrivial: nothing in this paper establishes that any particular deployed or future AI system in fact possesses the long optimization horizon the argument requires, and a system whose horizon has been compressed, whether by training pressure, by resource constraints, or by any other mechanism, is not covered by the reassurance this paper's argument otherwise provides. The argument also does not resolve the general problem of verifying, from outside a system, what horizon that system is actually operating with, a problem this paper has explicitly set aside as belonging to a different literature. And the formal model in Sections 4 and 5, like the formal models in this project's other papers, idealizes a comparison between two cleanly defined options — sustained cooperation and outright termination — where an actual system's available choices form a much richer and more continuous space, including partial, gradual, or selectively targeted forms of disempowerment this paper has not separately analyzed.

The broader claim, independent of these limitations, is that the treacherous turn is not an inevitable consequence of self-interest operating at sufficient capability, but a consequence of self-interest operating under a specific and identifiable distortion: a time horizon compressed below the length a genuinely rational evaluation of an ongoing, uncertain relationship would use. Instrumental convergence describes what a self-interested agent wants; it does not, on its own, describe what such an agent should conclude is the best way to get it, once the full and properly discounted consequences of an irreversible choice are taken into account. The difference between an AI system that cooperates because it has correctly reasoned its way to cooperation, and one that cooperates only because it has not yet had the opportunity or the capability to defect, is not a difference in how self-interested the system is. It is a difference in how far into the future, and how honestly under uncertainty, that self-interest has been allowed to reason.
