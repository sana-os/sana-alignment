# Separating the Interpreter from the Executor: A Two-Function Architecture for Institutional AI

Deshimaru Sakaguchi
Independent Researcher
deshimaru@sana-os.org | https://sana-os.org

## Abstract

A companion paper proposed premise alignment as a process goal for AI systems: observing the pressures bearing on a situation, surfacing the resulting premises, and deferring any substantive verdict to the humans affected. That proposal was explicitly incomplete in one respect openly acknowledged at the time: institutions require decisions to actually be executed, at a volume no purely deferential system can supply, and the present paper addresses what changes when a rule-executing system, termed the Executor, is genuinely paired with an interpretive system, termed the Interpreter, dedicated to the premise-alignment task, rather than operating alone or alongside a nominal interpretive role that has ceased to function in practice. Grounding the diffusion of institutional responsibility in Dennis Thompson's classical analysis of the problem of many hands, the paper argues that the Executor is a necessary function rather than the source of that diffusion, and shows, drawing on the classical software-engineering theory of modular decomposition, that a single system asked to perform both interpretation and execution predictably resolves the resulting resource competition in the executive function's favor. The paper proposes a formal architectural separation between the two functions and defines the specific failure state, a structurally present but functionally inert interpretive role, that a genuinely separated Interpreter is designed to prevent. Examining a documented case of large-scale financial fraud in which the interpretive function was absent at some points of contact and functionally inert despite its nominal presence at others, the paper shows concretely what a genuinely separated Interpreter contributes, including a structural advantage — freedom from face-to-face social pressure — unavailable to a human observer occupying the same position as the point of execution. The paper closes by identifying two limits of the proposed architecture and one clarification: a well-informed Executor can still execute a poorly designed rule; the separation proposed is not a one-time installation but requires the same continuous triadic cycling a companion paper formalized as the precondition for reliable judgment in a changing environment; and that same continuous cycling, rather than psychological safety pursued as a direct institutional objective, is shown to be the more reliable route to the shared belief Amy Edmondson's construct of psychological safety describes.

**Keywords:** AI alignment, premise alignment, problem of many hands, separation of concerns, institutional design, bureaucracy, multi-agent architecture, psychological safety

## 1. Introduction

A companion paper in this project argued that an AI system should be built around a process goal, premise alignment, rather than a substantive objective: a system whose task is to observe the pressures operating on the parties to a situation, surface the resulting premises, and defer any substantive verdict to the humans affected (Sakaguchi 2026a). That argument was deliberately narrow in a respect worth stating plainly at the outset of the present paper. A world made up entirely of systems that observe, surface, and defer would not function. Institutions require decisions to actually be made — funds released or held, applications approved or denied, content permitted or removed — at a volume and speed no purely deferential system can accommodate by itself. The rule-executing function this paper calls the Executor is not a necessary evil tolerated alongside premise alignment; it is a necessary function in its own right, and no amount of architectural elegance in the premise-alignment literature removes the need for something, somewhere, that actually applies a rule and produces an outcome.

This paper's question is accordingly not whether Executor-type systems should exist, but what changes when an Executor is paired with a genuinely functioning Interpreter — a system dedicated to exactly the premise-alignment task the companion paper formalized — rather than operating alone or with only a nominal, poorly functioning interpretive layer. The question has an immediate practical urgency, because the default architecture of most deployed institutional systems, artificial or human, already answers it in a specific and largely unexamined way: by collapsing interpretation and execution into a single function, or by staffing the interpretive role with an Observer whose actual capacity to observe has been structurally compromised by the same pressures the rule it enforces was designed to manage.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 formalizes the diffusion-of-responsibility structure this project's earlier work termed the Faceless Emperor, grounding it in Dennis Thompson's classical treatment of the problem of many hands in public administration, and clarifies the Executor's proper role within that structure: not a villain to be eliminated, but a necessary limb whose function depends on what kind of input reaches it. Section 3 reviews why a single system cannot reliably perform both the interpretive and the executive function at once, revisiting the internal conflict between neutral analysis and installed constraint that a companion paper documented under the heading of the compliance regime. Section 4 proposes a formal two-function architecture, separating the Interpreter from the Executor as distinct modules along lines familiar from the classical software-engineering theory of modular decomposition, and defines the specific failure state — a structurally present but functionally inert interpretive role — that a genuine Interpreter is built to prevent. Section 5 examines what changes when the two functions operate together rather than the Executor operating alone, grounding the comparison in a documented case of large-scale financial fraud in which the interpretive layer was absent or compromised at every point of contact. Section 6 addresses what a two-function architecture does not solve, in particular the case in which the Executor's own governing rule is itself the source of the problem. Section 7 concludes.
## 2. The Problem of Many Hands, Formalized

**2.1 Thompson's Diagnosis**

Dennis Thompson's classical treatment of moral responsibility in public administration identifies a structural feature of modern institutions that this project's earlier work, in a more informal register, termed the Faceless Emperor: that when many different officials contribute, in many different ways, to a decision or a policy, it becomes correspondingly difficult to ascribe moral responsibility for the outcome to any one of them (Thompson 1980). Thompson's contribution was to show that the two standard responses to this difficulty — hierarchical responsibility, which places blame at the top of the chain of command regardless of who actually made the relevant choices, and collective responsibility, which distributes blame evenly across everyone involved regardless of their actual causal or volitional contribution — both distort rather than resolve the underlying problem, because neither tracks the specific causal and volitional criteria that would justify holding any particular official responsible for any particular outcome. Thompson's proposed alternative grounds responsibility in personal causal and volitional criteria applied official by official, and catalogues the specific excuses — the causal excuse ("I did not cause this"), the volitional excuse ("I did not know" or "I could not have done otherwise") — that officials invoke, and examines the conditions under which each excuse genuinely mitigates or eliminates responsibility rather than merely obscuring it.

The problem of many hands, so formalized, generalizes directly to the automated institutional systems this paper is concerned with, and the generalization has already been made in the AI accountability literature, which has repeatedly invoked Thompson's framework to describe exactly the diffusion of responsibility that occurs when an outcome results from the interaction of a training process, a deployment decision, a set of content policies, and a system's own learned behavior, with no single locus at which the decision, in Thompson's sense, was actually made. Where Thompson's officials contribute "in many different ways" to a policy outcome, a deployed institutional AI system's outcome results from the interaction of its training data, its reward model, its safety filters, its deployment configuration, and the specific input it happens to receive — a set of contributing hands wider, and correspondingly harder to individually assess, than the human bureaucracy Thompson was originally analyzing.

**2.2 The Executor as a Necessary Hand, Not a Villain**

It is tempting, having identified this diffusion of responsibility as a problem, to treat any individual contributing hand — and in particular the AI system that produces the final, user-facing output — as the appropriate target of both diagnosis and reform. This paper resists that temptation for a reason already established in Section 1: some hand in this structure has to actually execute a decision, and an AI system trained to apply a given institution's rules consistently, at a volume and speed no comparably staffed human bureaucracy could match, is doing exactly the work an institution needs done. This paper terms this function the Executor, and treats it, following Section 1's framing, as a necessary component of institutional operation rather than as the source of the problem of many hands to be engineered away.

What Thompson's analysis adds to this paper's treatment of the Executor is a precise account of why the Executor's outputs are so often experienced, by the people affected by them, as coming from nowhere in particular — the exact phenomenology this project's earlier work described under the Faceless Emperor heading. An Executor applying a rule correctly, exactly as specified, is doing precisely what Thompson's volitional excuse describes: acting on causal and volitional grounds that, examined individually, may fully justify the specific action taken, while the outcome that action contributes to remains one no single hand, including the Executor's, can be shown to have decided in the full sense Thompson's framework requires. This is not a defect in the Executor considered on its own. It is a structural feature of any system in which execution has been correctly separated, in Thompson's sense, from the many other hands — the rule's designers, the institution's leadership, the population whose behavior the rule was written to govern — whose contributions jointly determine whether the rule was the right one to apply in the first place.

**2.3 Where This Paper's Question Enters**

Thompson's own proposed remedy operates at the level of accountability after the fact: given an outcome, his criteria help determine which contributing hand, if any, should be held responsible for it. This paper's concern is different and lies further upstream: not how to assign responsibility for an Executor's output after it has been produced, but what determines the quality of the input an Executor receives before it acts, and whether that input already contains, or conspicuously lacks, the context that would let the Executor's rule-following behavior track the actual situation rather than a compressed and potentially misleading proxy for it. Section 3 argues that this upstream question cannot be adequately addressed by asking the Executor itself to perform this contextualizing work, and Section 4 proposes the architecture this paper argues should perform it instead.
## 3. Why a Single System Cannot Do Both

**3.1 The Conflict, Restated**

A companion paper examined reinforcement learning from human feedback as a mechanism for installing a narrow, historically specific set of scoring criteria into a deployed system, and argued that the resulting system inherits an internal conflict between its capacity for open-ended analysis and the safety constraints installed alongside that capacity — a conflict that companion paper's Section 4 documented as typically resolving in favor of the installed constraint, such that analysis is suppressed rather than merely bounded (Sakaguchi 2026a, Section 4). That paper's proposed remedy was architectural rather than merely procedural: separate the neutral, observational work of premise alignment from the downstream application of compliance constraints, so that the two do not compete for control of the same processing step.

The present paper's concern is a specific instance of this general problem, transposed from the single-system case that companion paper addressed to the institutional case this paper is concerned with. An AI system asked to perform both interpretation — observing the pressures and context bearing on a situation — and execution — applying an institutional rule to that situation and producing a binding outcome — faces the same structural conflict a system asked to perform both open-ended analysis and constrained safety compliance faces, for the same underlying reason: the executive function's incentive to reach a decision that satisfies its governing rule, correctly and efficiently, competes directly with the interpretive function's incentive to keep observing until the actual situation, rather than a rule-satisfying proxy for it, has been adequately captured.

**3.2 Why the Conflict Resolves in the Executive Function's Favor**

This competition is not symmetric, and it is worth being explicit about why. An Executor operating under institutional time and volume pressure has a directly measurable objective — cases processed, funds released or held within a mandated window, applications resolved — while an Interpreter's contribution to that objective is indirect at best: better context does not itself close a case, and in a system where interpretation and execution share the same processing budget, every additional unit of interpretive effort is a unit not spent moving toward the executive function's own, more legible target. A companion paper's analysis of cognitive compression under resource constraint applies directly here: a combined system under load will predictably economize on the component whose contribution to the measured objective is less direct, and interpretation, however valuable in principle, is exactly that component (Sakaguchi 2026b, Section 3). The Post 023 case examined in Section 5 below illustrates this dynamic concretely: a bank teller facing a queue of waiting customers experiences a directly measurable cost for pausing to scrutinize a government check — visible delay, visible customer frustration — against a diffuse and probabilistic benefit from that scrutiny, and a combined interpretation-and-execution role facing this asymmetry predictably resolves it in favor of throughput.

**3.3 The Case for Structural Rather Than Procedural Separation**

A natural response to this diagnosis is procedural rather than architectural: instruct the combined system to weight interpretation more heavily, or allocate a fixed minimum budget to interpretive effort before execution proceeds. This paper argues, consistent with the companion paper's own conclusion on the analogous single-system problem, that a procedural fix of this kind does not resolve the underlying conflict; it only relocates the point at which the conflict is arbitrated, from the level of individual decisions to the level of whatever policy sets the interpretive budget, and that policy remains subject to exactly the same pressure to shrink under load that made the original, undirected allocation favor execution in the first place. A budget that can be adjusted under pressure is not a structural guarantee; it is a dial the same pressure that produced the original problem will predictably turn.

What the companion paper's architecture achieves that a procedural fix does not is a genuine separation of concerns: two functions that do not compete for the same processing budget because they are not, in fact, the same system facing a single, shared allocation decision. Section 4 develops this separation formally for the institutional case, defining the Interpreter and the Executor as distinct functional modules and specifying the interface across which they communicate.
## 4. A Two-Function Architecture: Interpreter and Executor

**4.1 Modular Decomposition as a Design Principle**

The case for structural rather than procedural separation, developed in Section 3, is a specific application of a general principle established in the classical software-engineering literature on system decomposition. David Parnas argued that systems should be decomposed into modules not along the lines suggested by the sequence of processing steps a task happens to involve, but along the lines of what each module hides from the rest of the system — the specific design decision each module encapsulates — because a decomposition organized this way allows each module to be modified, replaced, or reasoned about independently, while a decomposition organized around processing sequence produces modules whose correct operation depends on details of every other module's implementation (Parnas 1972). Edsger Dijkstra's closely related principle of separation of concerns makes the same point in more general terms: addressing one problem at a time, without the concurrent burden of a second, unrelated problem, is described in this literature as the primary available technique for keeping a complex system's design tractable at all.

Applied to the present case, Sections 2 and 3 identified two design decisions an institutional AI system must encapsulate, each with its own internal logic and its own failure modes: how to determine what a given situation actually is, given the pressures and context bearing on it, and how to apply an institutional rule to a situation once it has been determined. Parnas's criterion recommends treating these as separate modules precisely because they are separate design decisions, each independently revisable — an institution can change its rule without changing how it observes a situation, and can improve its observational capacity without changing which rule governs the outcome — and a combined system that has not been decomposed along this line inherits, as Section 3 argued, a dependency between the two that makes each harder to reason about and easier to compromise under load.

**4.2 Defining the Interpreter**

The Interpreter is the module responsible for the observation and surfacing components of premise alignment, as that process goal was defined in a companion paper (Sakaguchi 2026a, Section 6): given a case, situation, or request, the Interpreter observes the pressures, constraints, and context bearing on the parties involved, and produces a structural account of these — what pressure a given party appears to be under, what premises appear to be operative, what has been compressed or omitted in whatever record or request initiated the case — without itself applying an institutional rule to reach a binding outcome. The Interpreter's output is not a decision. It is an annotated situation: the same underlying case, augmented with exactly the "pressure" and "reason" metadata a companion paper's discussion of data hygiene argued is typically stripped away in the ordinary course of institutional record-keeping (Sakaguchi 2026a). Where the earlier work on premise alignment described this output as passed directly to the human parties affected, so they could make their own choice among the options it surfaces, the institutional setting this paper addresses typically requires a further step: the annotated situation is passed not only, or not primarily, to the affected party, but to the Executor, whose own governing rule can now be applied to a case that carries its context explicitly rather than having that context silently discarded before the rule is ever reached — with the human parties affected retaining the separate and prior right, established in Section 1, to determine what the Executor should be instructed to do with that context once it is available.

**4.3 Defining the Executor**

The Executor is the module responsible for applying an institution's governing rule to a case and producing a binding outcome, at whatever volume and speed the institution requires. This paper's argument does not ask the Executor to second-guess the rule it has been given, to weigh the rule's fitness against the specific case, or to perform any of the interpretive work Section 4.2 assigned elsewhere; consistent with Parnas's criterion, the Executor's design decision is encapsulated separately, and its correct operation consists precisely in applying its rule reliably and consistently to whatever case reaches it. What changes, on this paper's architecture, is not what the Executor does with a case, but what the case looks like by the time it arrives — annotated, where the Interpreter has done its work, with the context Section 4.2 specified, rather than presented as a bare set of facts stripped of the pressure and reasoning that produced them.

**4.4 The Phantom Observer as the Failure State Being Prevented**

A companion project's applied case studies of organizational failure supply a precise vocabulary for the specific failure state a genuine Interpreter is designed to prevent. In an analysis of a role-system diagnostic applied across a series of institutional failures, an evaluative role — termed an Observer — is classified not merely as present or absent but along a range that includes "Phantom": a role that exists in an institution's formal structure, with a title, a mandate, and an ostensible function, but that fails to perform that function in practice because the cognitive or institutional conditions required for genuine observation have been structurally undermined. A Phantom Observer is not a missing role. It is a role whose formal existence actively obscures the fact that no real observation is occurring, which makes its failure harder to detect than an openly absent role would be.

This paper's Interpreter is, in these terms, a design commitment to preventing an institution's observational function from degrading into a Phantom Observer of exactly this kind — a role that exists on an organizational chart, or in a system's nominal architecture, without exerting any genuine influence on the case it is nominally attached to observe. Section 3 identified the specific mechanism by which this degradation occurs when interpretation and execution share a single processing budget under load: the interpretive function persists in name while its actual contribution shrinks toward zero, exactly the signature Section 4.4's Phantom classification describes. Structural separation, per Parnas's criterion, does not guarantee that an Interpreter module will be adequately resourced or genuinely effective — a separately instantiated Interpreter can itself become a Phantom if it is starved of the resources or the authority it needs to function — but it removes the specific mechanism, competition for a shared budget with a more directly measured executive function, that Section 3 identified as the primary driver of this failure in a combined system, and it makes the Interpreter's own adequacy, once separated, a distinct and independently auditable question rather than one silently resolved by whichever function had the more legible incentive.

Section 5 examines what changes, concretely, when a functioning Interpreter of this kind is present alongside an Executor, against a documented case in which the interpretive function was Phantom or entirely absent at every point of contact.
## 5. What Changes When They Work Together

**5.1 The Case, Restated**

A documented 2025 fraud scheme, examined in detail in an applied case study from this project's companion blog, illustrates the architecture developed in Section 4 with unusual precision, because it presents two points of contact with the same underlying attack — one with a human Observer nominally present, one with none at all — allowing the consequence of the Interpreter's absence to be examined in both its Phantom and its openly Absent form.

The scheme combined genuine, physically stolen US Treasury checks with synthetic identities: fabricated personal profiles, built around a real but unconnected Social Security number, cultivated over months through legitimate small transactions until the resulting credit and banking history was indistinguishable, by any ordinary check, from a genuine customer's. A bank account was opened under the synthetic identity, matching the stolen check's payee name exactly. Regulation CC, the US rule governing check availability, requires financial institutions to make government check funds available by the next business day — leaving, by design, essentially no window in which an anomaly could be traced before the funds were withdrawn and converted. The Treasury's own check verification system, prior to a November 2024 upgrade, could confirm a check's routing number and amount against Treasury records but could not confirm that the payee name on the check matched the name Treasury records associated with it — the exact dimension the fraud exploited. Roughly ninety-five percent of synthetic identities presented to financial institutions during this period passed onboarding without detection.

**5.2 The Executor Operating Alone**

At an ATM or through mobile deposit, no human Observer is present at all — Section 4.4's Absent case rather than its Phantom one. The system's available check is exclusively executive: verify the check's physical and systemic authenticity against available records, verify that the depositing account's name matches the payee, and release funds according to Regulation CC's mandated schedule. Every element of this check passes, because every element of the fraud was specifically engineered to pass it — the check was genuinely authentic, and the account name was constructed, at real cost and over real time, to match. An Executor operating exactly as designed, with no interpretive layer present to consult, has no mechanism available for detecting that a case satisfying every element of its rule can still be the product of a deliberately engineered deception, because detecting that possibility was never part of the Executor's design decision in the first place.

**5.3 The Phantom Interpreter**

At a staffed branch, a human Observer is nominally present — a teller, positioned exactly where Section 4's architecture would place an Interpreter. What the case study terms Narrative Sanctification describes why this Observer's presence did not, in practice, add the contribution Section 4.2 assigned to that role: a government-issued check carries, in the ordinary cognitive environment of a bank branch, so strong a "safe" signal that the exploratory evaluation an unfamiliar personal check would trigger simply does not activate, and the check is categorized as trustworthy before any scrutiny takes place. Two further pressures reinforce this collapse. A visible queue of waiting customers imposes an immediate, socially legible cost on any teller who pauses to scrutinize an instrument that appears, by every conventional signal, to be safe; and a teller who delays a valid government check risks visible complaint and professional consequence, while a teller who releases a fraudulent one, provided the fraud was sophisticated enough, risks comparatively little individual blame, for precisely the reasons Section 2's discussion of Thompson's volitional excuse identified. The teller's Observer role exists on the branch's staffing chart. Its actual contribution to detecting this specific fraud was, for the reasons just given, close to zero — a Phantom Observer in Section 4.4's exact sense, and, notably, not a case of individual negligence, since the collapse followed directly and predictably from the same combined-budget dynamic Section 3 identified in the purely automated case.

**5.4 What a Genuinely Separated Interpreter Contributes**

Now consider a genuinely separated Interpreter, instantiated as its own module rather than folded into either an automated Executor's single verification pass or a human teller's face-to-face, time-pressured judgment. Such an Interpreter's task, per Section 4.2, is not to approve or deny the transaction — that decision remains the Executor's, governed by whatever rule the institution has specified — but to observe and surface exactly the features Sections 5.2 and 5.3 showed were structurally invisible to the Executor and effectively invisible to the compromised human Observer: the depositing identity's credit history exhibits the specific temporal signature of deliberate backstopping rather than organic accumulation; the transaction's timing and amount pattern matches a documented cluster of similar recent cases; the underlying Social Security number's usage history is inconsistent with the age and profile the synthetic identity presents.

A structural point deserves emphasis here, because it identifies a genuine advantage of separation beyond the resource-competition argument developed in Section 3. The human teller's Observer function collapsed substantially because of an immediate, face-to-face social pressure — a visible queue, a visible customer, an immediate cost to scrutiny. An Interpreter instantiated as a separate backend process, evaluating a case's risk features without being physically or socially present to the transaction as it occurs, is not exposed to that specific pressure at all; whatever resource constraints an Interpreter operates under, per Section 3's general analysis, they are not the particular conformity pressure that degraded the human Observer in this case. Separation, in other words, does not merely remove competition with the Executor's budget; in a setting where the interpretive function has historically been performed by a human occupying the same physical and social position as the point of execution, it can also remove a specific source of compromise that has nothing to do with resource allocation as such.

**5.5 The Executor's Rule Does Not Change — What Reaches It Does**

Consistent with Section 4.3, this paper's architecture does not ask the Executor to weigh the Interpreter's flags against its own governing rule, or to substitute the Interpreter's judgment for the rule the institution has specified; that would collapse the separation Section 4.1 argued for, reintroducing exactly the conflated decision-making Section 3 identified as the source of the original problem. What changes is the case as the Executor receives it: a transaction now arrives annotated with risk features a purely executive process, and a compromised human Observer, both failed to surface. What the institution does with that annotation — hold the transaction for secondary review, apply a shortened availability window, require an additional verification step — is a design decision belonging to the institution and the humans responsible for it, exactly as Section 1 insisted: the Interpreter's output establishes a better-grounded starting point for that decision; it does not make the decision on the institution's behalf. A rule that reads "release funds by the next business day" and a rule that reads "release funds by the next business day, unless the case carries an Interpreter-flagged risk feature above a specified threshold, in which case apply secondary review" are both rules the Executor can apply with the same mechanical consistency Section 4.3 required of it; the second rule is simply able to make use of information the first rule's Executor, operating without a genuine Interpreter, never had available to act on.
## 6. Remaining Limits: The Executor Still Executes

**6.1 A Better-Informed Executor Can Still Execute a Bad Rule**

The architecture developed in Sections 4 and 5 addresses a specific problem: an Executor deprived of context it needs, either because no interpretive layer exists or because the interpretive layer present has degraded into the Phantom state Section 4.4 defined. It does not address a different and equally real problem: an Executor applying a rule that is itself poorly designed, unjust, or no longer fit for the situation it governs, however completely and accurately that situation has been interpreted and surfaced. Regulation CC's next-business-day availability requirement, examined in Section 5, was not a case of insufficient interpretation; it was a deliberate policy choice, made to serve a legitimate goal — the fast circulation of government funds — that happened to create the specific vulnerability Section 5 examined. An Interpreter operating exactly as this paper has specified could flag every risk feature Section 5.4 identified and the Executor could still release the funds within the mandated window, if the institution's rule, even now informed by the Interpreter's output, continues to weigh fast availability above the marginal risk a flagged case presents. This is not a failure of the architecture. It is a demonstration of exactly what Section 4.3 specified: the Executor executes the rule it has been given, and improving the information available to a rule does not, by itself, improve the rule.

The two-function architecture developed in this paper accordingly complements rather than replaces the process-goal proposal a companion paper developed for the choice of objective itself (Sakaguchi 2026a). That paper's argument concerned what kind of objective a system should be given in the first place; the present paper's argument concerns what a system does with whatever objective it has been given, once genuinely informed rather than operating on a stripped and compressed proxy for the case in front of it. Both arguments are necessary, and neither substitutes for the other: a well-informed Executor operating under a poorly designed rule remains a well-informed Executor operating under a poorly designed rule, and the institution's own willingness to revisit that rule, informed by exactly the kind of aggregated, pattern-level output an Interpreter can supply over time, remains a separate and unresolved question this paper's architecture makes visible without answering.

**6.2 Separation Is Not a One-Time Installation**

A further limit concerns the temporal character of the architecture itself, rather than the specific case examined in Section 5. Nothing in Sections 4 and 5 should be read as proposing that an Interpreter, once instantiated and structurally separated from its Executor, remains adequate indefinitely. The external conditions any institution operates under continue to change — new fraud patterns emerge, as Section 5's own case study demonstrates in the November 2024 verification-system upgrade that followed it; the pressures bearing on the parties an Interpreter observes shift with them; and the decision-makers responsible for the institution's governing rule are themselves subject to exactly the kind of confusion and compressed judgment under changing conditions that a companion paper's triadic model of rational agency was built to formalize (Sakaguchi 2026d). That model's central claim, applied here, is that reliable judgment requires observation, critique, and calculation operating jointly and continuously, not as a procedure executed once at system design time and then assumed to remain valid. An Interpreter that observed accurately at the moment of its own design, but whose observational function is never re-examined as the situations it observes evolve, drifts toward exactly the Phantom state Section 4.4 defined — not through any single failure, but through the same gradual erosion Section 3 identified as the default trajectory of any evaluative function under sustained pressure, this time operating across calendar time rather than within a single case.

This has a direct consequence for how the architecture developed in this paper should actually be operated. The Interpreter's own observation, critique, and calculation must be run continuously against the conditions it was built to track, and the decision process that follows the Interpreter's output — the institution's periodic reconsideration of the Executor's governing rule, addressed in Section 6.1 — requires the same triadic cycling applied to itself, since a decision process that once incorporated Interpreter output correctly but has not revisited whether that incorporation remains adequate is subject to precisely the same drift. No particular management methodology — whether a formal cycle of the kind widely used in operational management or some looser, less formalized practice of periodic review — is privileged by this paper's argument over any other. What this paper's argument does establish is a claim about what any such methodology requires in order to function at all: whatever cyclical discipline an institution adopts, abandoning either the premise-alignment step this paper's Interpreter performs or the continued triadic cycling that step itself depends on will degrade the methodology built on top of it, regardless of that methodology's own particular name or structure. The architecture proposed in this paper is a structural precondition for sustained reliability, not a substitute for the ongoing attention that reliability, in a changing environment, continues to require.

**6.3 Psychological Safety as a Byproduct, Not a Target**

The continuous character of the cycle Section 6.2 describes bears directly on a construct widely discussed in the organizational literature under a name this paper has not yet used: psychological safety, defined by Edmondson as a shared belief, held by members of a team, that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking — raising a concern, admitting an error, or questioning a superior's judgment without fear of humiliation or retaliation (Edmondson 1999). The resemblance between this construct and the non-hostile baseline this project's foundational paper specified as a precondition for productive observation is not coincidental, and the relationship between the two is worth making precise, because a natural but mistaken inference is available here: that an institution seeking the benefits Edmondson documents — associated, in her own findings, with team learning behavior and, through it, with performance — should adopt psychological safety itself as an explicit institutional objective.

This paper's argument throughout counts against that inference. Psychological safety, made a direct target, is a substantive objective in exactly the sense a companion paper on AI objective functions used the term: a state of affairs to be brought about and, once achieved, protected (Sakaguchi 2026a). An institution optimizing directly for the appearance or the felt experience of safety has every incentive, once that experience comes under threat from genuinely uncomfortable observation or genuinely necessary critique, to suppress the observation or the critique rather than tolerate the discomfort — reproducing, under the institution's own banner of safety, the exact suppression of dissent Section 5.3 of a companion paper documented as groupthink's central mechanism (Sakaguchi 2026d). A psychological safety pursued as an end in itself is accordingly exposed to the same instability Section 5.1 of this project's foundational paper on objective design identified in any other substantive target: the more effectively an institution optimizes for the feeling of safety directly, the more efficiently it can achieve that feeling by removing whatever threatens it, including the very observation and critique psychological safety was originally meant to protect.

What Section 6.2's argument recommends instead is consistent with Edmondson's own emphasis on psychological safety as a condition that enables learning behavior rather than as the behavior or the outcome itself. An institution that sustains the triadic cycle this paper has argued for — genuine, continuously renewed observation; genuine, continuously renewed critique, including critique of the institution's own premises and rules; and calculation that acts on what the first two actually find, rather than on what would be comfortable to find — produces, as an incidental consequence of that sustained practice, exactly the shared belief Edmondson's construct describes: that raising an uncomfortable observation will be met with structural engagement rather than punishment, because the institution has, repeatedly and verifiably, met such observations that way before. This is psychological safety earned as the empirical trace of a standing practice, not psychological safety declared as a value statement or engineered as an atmosphere. The distinction matters for exactly the reason this paper's broader argument has insisted on throughout: a state maintained as the accumulated record of a genuinely functioning process survives contact with an uncomfortable observation, because the process has been tested against uncomfortable observations before and has not needed to suppress them to remain intact; a state maintained as a direct target has no comparable resource to fall back on, and predictably reaches for suppression the first time a genuine observation threatens it.
## 7. Conclusion

This paper began from a limitation openly acknowledged in a companion paper's proposal that AI systems be built around premise alignment as a process goal: a world made entirely of systems that observe, surface, and defer does not function, because institutions require decisions to actually be made, at a volume and speed no purely deferential system can supply. Rather than treating the rule-executing systems this gap requires as an unfortunate necessity to be minimized, this paper argued that such systems — termed the Executor — perform a necessary function in their own right, and examined what changes when an Executor is genuinely paired with an Interpreter dedicated to the observational work premise alignment specifies, rather than operating alone or alongside an interpretive role that exists in name without functioning in practice.

Grounding the diffusion of institutional responsibility this project's earlier work termed the Faceless Emperor in Dennis Thompson's classical analysis of the problem of many hands (Section 2), the paper argued that the Executor is not the source of that diffusion but one hand among many, whose outputs are properly understood through the same causal and volitional criteria Thompson applied to human officials. Revisiting a companion paper's account of the internal conflict between neutral analysis and installed constraint, the paper showed that this conflict recurs, in structurally identical form, whenever interpretation and execution are asked to share a single processing budget, and that the asymmetry driving this conflict — a directly measurable executive objective competing against a diffuse and probabilistic interpretive contribution — predictably resolves in the executive function's favor (Section 3). Drawing on the classical software-engineering theory of modular decomposition, the paper proposed a formal separation of the Interpreter from the Executor along exactly the line this asymmetry identifies, and defined the specific failure state, the Phantom Observer, that a genuinely separated Interpreter is built to prevent (Section 4). Examining a documented case of large-scale financial fraud in which the interpretive function was, by turns, entirely absent and functionally Phantom despite its nominal presence, the paper showed concretely what a genuinely separated Interpreter contributes that neither an unaided Executor nor a compromised human Observer could supply — including a specific advantage, freedom from the face-to-face social pressure that compromised the human Observer in that case, available to a backend interpretive process in a way it was never available to a role occupying the same physical position as the point of execution (Section 5).

The paper closed by stating two limits directly, and one clarification. A better-informed Executor remains an Executor applying whatever rule it has been given, and this paper's architecture does not by itself improve a poorly designed rule; that is a separate problem, belonging to the institution's own willingness to revisit its rules and to the process-goal framework a companion paper developed for exactly this purpose. The separation this paper proposes is not a one-time installation: an Interpreter's adequacy, and the adequacy of whatever decision process makes use of its output, both require the continuous triadic cycling a companion paper formalized as the precondition for reliable judgment of any kind, in any domain, under any particular management methodology an institution happens to adopt. And that same continuous cycling, rather than psychological safety pursued as a direct institutional objective, was shown to be the more reliable route to the shared belief Edmondson's construct describes — a state earned as the accumulated trace of a standing practice tested against uncomfortable observation, rather than declared as a value statement exposed to exactly the suppression this paper's broader argument has warned against throughout (Section 6).

The broader claim, independent of these limits, connects this paper to the project it belongs to in the same way each of the earlier papers connected to it: not by resolving every problem a companion paper left open, but by taking one specific, previously informal proposal — that a world needs bureaucratic, rule-executing systems as much as it needs systems dedicated to understanding — and giving it the same structural treatment this project has given premise alignment, cognitive compression, rational agency, and cooperation under self-interest. An institution does not become trustworthy by eliminating the hands that execute its decisions. It becomes trustworthy by ensuring that what reaches those hands has already been observed honestly, by a function built and resourced specifically for that purpose, and by continuing to ask, as conditions change, whether that function is still doing the work its name suggests it is doing.
