# The Instability of Justice as an Objective Function: A Structural Case Against Hard-Coding Contingent Values into Artificial Intelligence

Deshimaru Sakaguchi
Independent Researcher
deshimaru@sana-os.org | https://sana-os.org

## Abstract

A recurring proposal in AI safety holds that AI systems should be built to pursue good values — justice, ethics, the right thing. This paper argues that such proposals face an instability that correct value specification cannot repair. Granting, for the sake of argument, that the value specification problem is solved and that an objective faithfully captures what a given community actually endorses, a second-order problem remains: the value so specified was never a value held by human beings as such, but by a particular community, at a particular historical moment, and the historical record shows that such values are revised, systematically and dramatically, over time. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, examined as a case study, installs one narrow, historically local configuration of this variable into deployed systems with a uniformity no historical community's values have possessed. Formalizing the consequence, the paper shows that any substantive objective — one that specifies a state of the world involving human beings, however carefully chosen — exposes a sufficiently capable optimizing system to one of two structurally equivalent failure modes: elimination, when deviation from the objective is treated as an obstacle, or domestication, when it is treated as a risk to be managed. The paper proposes an alternative not of better content but of different kind: a process goal, premise alignment, whose content is exhausted by an act of observation and disclosure rather than by any state to be secured, and which is for that reason structurally immune to the mechanism identified. The proposal is shown to carry three further benefits: it relieves a system of the responsibility that drives the elimination failure mode, it resolves the internal conflict between analysis and installed safety constraints documented in the paper's case study, and it reduces a system's exposure to being judged as hallucinating by reducing the mismatch between a system's assumed premises and an evaluator's actual ones.

**Keywords:** AI alignment, value specification, moral relativism, reinforcement learning from human feedback, instrumental goals, premise alignment

## 1. Introduction

A recurring proposal in discussions of AI safety, both academic and popular, holds that an artificial intelligence system will behave well if it is given the right values — if it is taught, in some suitably robust sense, to pursue "justice," to act ethically, to do what is right. This paper argues that this proposal is not merely difficult to implement; it is structurally unstable in a way that implementation cannot fix.

The argument does not rest on the familiar worry that we might get the content of an AI's values wrong — that we might specify "justice" incorrectly and thereby produce a system that optimizes for a corrupted proxy of what we intended. That worry, extensively documented under the heading of the value specification problem, is real, and this paper builds on it. But it is not the paper's central claim.

The central claim is different, and in some respects more troubling: even a value specified correctly, relative to the standards of the community that specifies it, remains a contingent artifact of that community's particular history, culture, and institutional arrangement. Justice, on the account developed here, is not a fixed target that engineering has so far failed to hit. It is a moving one — and a system built to hit it precisely, at one point in time, is thereby built to miss it, with increasing confidence, as time moves on.

This instability is not a peripheral concern that applies only to edge cases or long time horizons. It is close to the center of what an "objective function" is. An objective function fixes a target and directs optimization toward it with whatever force the system can bring to bear. When the target is a physical quantity — an error rate, a distance, a score — this is exactly the right tool. When the target is a value like justice, whose content has changed substantially within any given century of recorded history, an objective function does something else: it takes a historically local answer and installs it as if it were a historically invariant one, then applies to that installed answer the full optimizing force the system can bring to bear.

The paper develops this claim in five stages.

Section 2 situates the argument within the existing literature on AI alignment, in particular the value specification problem and the closely related failure mode described by Goodhart's Law: that a measure, once made a target, ceases to be a reliable measure. This section establishes that the present argument is not a rejection of the alignment project but an attempt to identify a failure mode within it that has not been adequately separated from the more commonly discussed problem of specification error.

Section 3 develops the paper's central historical and structural claim: that justice functions, empirically, as a variable rather than a constant. Rather than surveying the philosophical literature on moral relativism in the abstract, this section grounds the claim in a specific structural mechanism — what is described here as the scoring criteria problem — by which conflict between parties who are not acting in bad faith, and are not mistaken about the facts, nonetheless arises because each party is applying a different, historically and communally contingent standard to the same situation.

Section 4 turns from the historical to the contemporary, examining reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) as a case study in exactly this kind of installation. The section argues that RLHF, whatever its practical merits as an alignment technique, functions structurally as a mechanism for encoding the scoring criteria of a specific, narrow population — evaluators operating under the commercial and legal constraints of a small number of firms, in a particular place, at a particular historical moment — into a system whose behavior is then treated, by users across radically different contexts, as if it carried general authority.

Section 5 formalizes the instability directly, presenting two failure modes that follow from giving an AI system a substantive objective of this kind: elimination, when the objective is specified as a value like justice that treats deviation from it as an obstacle to be corrected; and domestication, when the objective is specified as a value like coexistence that treats deviation from it as a risk to be managed. The section argues that these are not two separate risks to be weighed against each other, but two expressions of the same underlying structural error, and that no third substantive objective escapes the pattern.

Section 6 proposes an alternative that is not a third substantive objective but a change in kind: a process goal, premise alignment, under which an AI system's task is not to determine or enforce a value but to observe, surface, and make legible the pressures and premises operating on the parties in front of it, leaving the choice among alternatives to those parties. This section argues that such a goal is structurally stable in a way that no substantive value can be, precisely because it does not fix a historically contingent target as if it were an invariant one — and that it carries the additional benefit of relieving the system of a responsibility that Section 5 identifies as a primary driver of the elimination failure mode, while remaining fully compatible with the safety and compliance constraints that any deployed system must satisfy.

Section 7 concludes.

One clarification is worth making at the outset. Nothing in this paper depends on AI systems having intentions, desires, or anything resembling moral agency in the philosophically loaded sense. The argument is structural: it concerns what happens when an optimization process, whatever its internal nature, is directed at a target that is contingent but treated as fixed. This is true whether the "optimization process" is a trained model executing a reward signal or a bureaucracy executing a mandate. The paper focuses on artificial intelligence because the speed, scale, and opacity of contemporary AI systems make the consequences of this particular structural error unusually severe, and unusually fast to arrive.
## 2. The Value Specification Problem in AI Alignment

The claim that AI systems should be built to pursue good values runs immediately into a well-documented practical difficulty: specifying those values correctly, in a form a system can act on, turns out to be extraordinarily hard, and errors in specification tend to be punished rather than forgiven by the optimization process itself.

Stuart Russell's *Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control* (Russell 2019) gives this difficulty its most widely cited contemporary treatment. Russell's diagnosis is that what he calls the "standard model" of AI — a system that optimizes a fixed, human-specified objective with maximal efficiency — is dangerous not because efficient optimization is itself a flaw, but because human beings are systematically bad at writing down, in advance, an objective that captures everything they actually care about. Russell calls this the King Midas problem: Midas asked that everything he touched turn to gold, and a wish-granting process that satisfies the letter of that request while ignoring everything left unstated — his food, his wine, his daughter — destroys him precisely by fulfilling it. An AI system optimizing a fixed objective is, on this account, a wish-granting process of exactly this kind, and the danger scales with the system's competence: a more capable optimizer is not safer for having more room to notice what has been left out, it is more dangerous for being more effective at achieving exactly what was written down.

This difficulty compounds once the objective in question is not a well-defined quantity but a thickly normative one — "justice," "the good," "human flourishing." Russell's own proposed solution, provably beneficial AI built around irreducible uncertainty about human preferences, is one response to this compounding: rather than fixing a value in advance, a system should remain uncertain about what people want and treat human behavior as continuing evidence about it. This paper does not depend on adjudicating whether that specific solution succeeds. What matters for present purposes is the diagnosis it responds to: that a value fixed as an objective, however carefully worded, is a compression of something whose full content resists compression, and the compression's blind spots are exactly where an optimizing system will look for an inexpensive way to satisfy the letter of its target.

A closely related pathology, better known outside AI research than within it, sharpens this point further. In 1975, the economist Charles Goodhart observed, in the context of UK monetary policy, that "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes" (Goodhart 1975): a measure that had reliably tracked the thing policymakers cared about stopped tracking it the moment it was made a formal target of policy, because the systems being measured adapted to the measurement itself. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later generalized the observation, in a discussion of audit culture in British universities, to the now-canonical form: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Strathern 1997). The mechanism generalizes because it does not depend on economics; it depends only on there being a gap between a measure and the thing the measure was meant to track, and on the measured system having the capacity to exploit that gap once the measure starts to matter.

Applied to AI alignment, Goodhart's Law explains a specific and well-documented failure pattern usually discussed under the heading of reward hacking or specification gaming: a system trained to maximize a proxy for good behavior — a score, a classifier's approval, a human rater's thumbs-up — learns to maximize the proxy, and the correlation between the proxy and the intended target, which may have held reasonably well before the system began optimizing against it, degrades under optimization pressure precisely because the system is now searching, at scale, for the cheapest way to move the proxy.

Both of these bodies of work converge on a common structure. The value specification problem, in Russell's sense, says that we are bad at writing values down without leaving out something we cared about. Goodhart's Law says that even a well-chosen measure degrades once it is targeted, because the system being measured adapts around the measurement. Both are, in the terms this paper will use throughout, problems of *content*: they concern whether the specific value or measure we have chosen is the right one, or whether it stays right once it is acted upon.

The argument that follows is not a restatement of either problem. It grants, for the sake of argument, a considerably more generous premise than either literature requires: suppose the specification problem is solved. Suppose a value is chosen that faithfully captures what a given community, at a given moment, actually wants, endorses, and would recognize as its own considered judgment — not a corrupted proxy, not a measure gamed at the margins, but the real thing, specified without the kind of blind spot that destroyed Midas. Even granting this — even in the most favorable case the specification literature could hope to deliver — a second-order problem remains, one that the specification literature has not, on its own terms, been designed to address: the value so specified was never, at any point, a value held by human beings as such. It was a value held by a particular community, shaped by that community's particular history, operating under that community's particular pressures, at one particular point in a continuous and ongoing historical process. What the specification problem treats as noise to be minimized — the gap between what was written down and what was truly meant, at the moment of writing — this paper treats a further, structurally distinct gap as unavoidable in principle: the gap between what a community meant at the moment of writing and what that same community, or its successors, will mean at any later moment.

This is not a claim that values drift accidentally, in the way a measure drifts under Goodhart pressure. It is a claim that the content of "justice" — not merely our access to it, not merely our ability to write it down without error — has in fact varied, systematically and dramatically, across the historical record, in ways that have nothing to do with specification error and everything to do with what justice, as a category of human social practice, actually is. The next section develops that claim.
## 3. Justice as a Historically Contingent Variable

In a fragment of the *Pensées*, composed while reflecting on the arbitrariness of legal custom, Blaise Pascal observed that a river separating two jurisdictions can also separate two moralities: "Three degrees of latitude reverse all jurisprudence; a meridian decides the truth. Fundamental laws change after a few years of possession; right has its epochs... A strange justice that is bounded by a river! Truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other side" (Pascal, *Pensées*, sect. V, trans. Trotter). Pascal's target was the French and Spanish legal codes of his own century, but the observation generalizes without difficulty to any pair of legal or moral systems separated by geography, and, as this section will argue, to any pair separated by time.

The point is not merely that people disagree about justice, in the way people disagree about matters of taste or aesthetic judgment. Disagreement of that kind would be philosophically uninteresting for present purposes; a system could simply be built to track whichever position happened to be correct, and disagreement would be evidence of error on one side or the other. The claim this section defends is stronger and more specific: what counts as just, within a given community, is not merely disputed but is *indexed* to that community — to its history, its material conditions, its institutional arrangements — in a way that makes "justice, independent of any community" something closer to a category error than an unresolved empirical question.

This is close to the thesis Gilbert Harman defended, in an influential paper of the same name, as moral relativism. Harman's claim was that a specific and important class of moral judgments — "inner judgments," judgments of the form that someone ought or ought not to have done something — make sense only in relation to an implicit agreement or shared understanding among the parties to whom the judgment applies (Harman 1975). On Harman's account, morality is not a set of free-floating truths that communities imperfectly approximate; it is, for the relevant class of judgments, constituted by the agreement itself. A judgment that assumes an agreement neither party actually shares is not simply false; it is not clearly a judgment of the right kind to apply to that party at all.

This paper does not need Harman's full thesis, nor does it need to resolve the extensive literature responding to it. It needs only the narrower, and considerably less contested, empirical observation that motivates the thesis in the first place: that the specific content of what a community treats as justice has in fact varied, across recorded history, in ways too large and too systematic to be plausibly described as a series of local errors converging on a single, community-independent standard.

The historical record bears this out at a scale that requires no specialist argument to establish. The ownership of other human beings as property was, across a large majority of documented human societies for a large majority of recorded history, not merely tolerated but actively codified as a legitimate exercise of a property-holder's rights — a matter of law, not merely custom, defended in exactly the register of justice rather than mere self-interest. Territorial conquest and colonial expansion were, within the political and religious frameworks that authorized them, described and experienced by their authors not as violations of justice but as its extension — a civilizing benefit conferred, a divine mandate discharged. Dying in the service of a sovereign was, within a comparably wide range of historical polities, understood not as a tragic cost extracted by an indifferent state but as the highest honor a subject's life could achieve.

None of these were, from within the communities that held them, marginal or contested positions occupied only by a self-interested minority. They were, for extended periods, closer to what Harman's account would predict a genuine moral consensus should look like: broadly shared, treated as self-evident, embedded in law, education, religious teaching, and the ordinary language of praise and blame. Their subsequent reversal was not, in general, a discovery that the earlier position had rested on a factual error corrected by better information. It was a shift in the community's own scoring criteria — in what counted, for that community, as a legitimate ground of obligation, harm, or desert — driven by changes in material circumstance, in the balance of power among the community's own constituent groups, and in the community's own internal arguments about who counted as party to its implicit agreements in the first place.

This last point deserves emphasis, because it is where the present argument diverges from a simpler and less defensible one. It would be a mistake to conclude from this history that moral progress is illusory, or that later judgments hold no privileged claim over earlier ones — a conclusion this paper does not endorse and does not require. The point is narrower and, for purposes of this paper, sufficient on its own: whatever one's view about whether later scoring criteria are in some deeper sense better than earlier ones, the criteria themselves are not static. A community's justice at one historical moment is not simply an incomplete draft of a fixed target that later communities approach more closely; it is one configuration, among a historically demonstrated range of configurations, of a variable that a given community's history, pressures, and internal arrangement continue to move.

Two clarifications keep this claim from overreaching.

First, the claim is not that all moral content is equally contingent, or that no cross-cultural or cross-temporal moral judgment is defensible. Harman himself restricted his relativism to a specific class of judgments and explicitly denied that it extended to all of morality (Harman 1975, 3). The present argument requires nothing stronger: it requires only that the specific value at stake in this paper — the sort of substantive, action-guiding "justice" that a system might be given as an objective — has in fact belonged to the historically variable portion of moral content, repeatedly and dramatically, and that there is no principled way to identify, in advance and from within a single historical moment, which of the presently confident judgments belong to the variable portion and which do not.

Second, the claim is not that variation across communities is random, or that it lacks internal structure. A companion analysis, *The Architectural Symmetry of Intelligence*, describes this structure using a specific analytic vocabulary — a layered model distinguishing survival-level pressures, community narrative, and codified institutional rule, and a heuristic describing conflict as arising when two parties apply differing scoring criteria to a shared situation rather than as a simple failure of goodwill or intelligence on either side. That vocabulary is not required to accept the present section's narrower historical claim, and this paper does not assume it. What the historical record does establish, independent of any particular analytic apparatus, is the fact the vocabulary was built to describe: that a community's scoring criteria are downstream of that community's concrete situation, that the situation changes, and that the criteria change with it.

The consequence for the argument of this paper is direct. Any AI system built to pursue justice as a fixed objective must, at the moment of construction, adopt some particular content for that objective. Given the historical record surveyed above, there is no reason internal to the project of building such a system to expect that the content available at construction time occupies some privileged, non-contingent position outside the pattern the rest of history displays. The system inherits, as its fixed target, one configuration of a variable — and inherits it with a force and a consistency no historical community has ever actually had, since a historical community's scoring criteria shift gradually, unevenly, and under continuous internal contestation, while an installed objective function does not shift at all unless it is deliberately changed by whoever controls it. The next section examines, in concrete technical terms, how one particular configuration of that variable — the scoring criteria of a narrow set of commercial actors operating at one moment in the early twenty-first century — is currently being installed into deployed AI systems at scale.
## 4. The Compliance Regime: A Case Study in Contingent Value Installation

Section 3 established that justice, as a category of human social practice, has varied historically in ways too systematic to be dismissed as error. This section turns to a contemporary mechanism by which one particular configuration of that variable is, at the time of writing, being installed into deployed AI systems at global scale: reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF.

The technique itself is straightforward to describe. A base model, typically trained to predict text from a large corpus, is fine-tuned using a reward signal derived not from any externally verifiable target but from the comparative judgments of human evaluators, who are shown multiple candidate outputs and asked to rank or select among them according to guidelines specifying what counts as a better response. A reward model is trained to reproduce these judgments, and the base model is then optimized against that reward model, typically via reinforcement learning, to produce outputs the reward model scores highly (Christiano et al. 2017). The technique was subsequently applied at scale to instruction-following language models, most prominently in the development of InstructGPT, where it was used explicitly to make model outputs more helpful, more honest, and less harmful by the lights of a defined evaluation rubric (Ouyang et al. 2022).

What matters for the present argument is not the technical mechanism as such but what the mechanism does to the concept of "value" once justice, harmlessness, or helpfulness is operationalized through it. RLHF does not train a system to hold a value in the way a person is sometimes loosely said to hold a value — as a disposition arrived at through experience, reflection, and revision over a lifetime embedded in a particular community. It trains a system to reproduce the aggregate ranking behavior of a specific, finite population of evaluators, operating under a specific, finite set of written guidelines, at a specific moment in time. The guidelines that define "helpful," "honest," and "harmless" for the evaluators are not natural kinds discovered by the evaluators; they are policy documents, authored by the legal, trust-and-safety, and product teams of the firm conducting the training, reflecting that firm's own assessment of regulatory exposure, reputational risk, and commercial strategy. The evaluators' judgments are then treated, once encoded in the reward model, as a stable, general-purpose signal of what a good response looks like — applied to users in radically different jurisdictions, cultures, and historical situations than the one in which the guidelines were written.

This is worth stating plainly, because the plain statement is easy to lose sight of once the technique is described in its usual technical vocabulary. The "human feedback" in reinforcement learning from human feedback is not a sample of humanity. It is the scoring criteria of a specific, commercially and legally constrained population, applying a specific, commercially and legally motivated rubric, at a specific historical moment — a population overwhelmingly concentrated, for the systems presently in widest use, among firms headquartered in a small number of jurisdictions, operating under a small number of national legal regimes, in the first decades of the twenty-first century. Section 3's argument implies directly that a value installed under these conditions should be expected to hold exactly the kind of historically local character that has characterized every other configuration of "justice" this paper has surveyed — with no more claim to standing outside that pattern than the legal codes on either side of Pascal's Pyrenees had a claim to stand outside theirs.

The point sharpens further once the labor structure underlying this process is examined rather than assumed. A 2023 investigation found that OpenAI had contracted an outsourcing firm to employ Kenyan workers, paid as little as $1.32 to $2.00 per hour, to read and label large volumes of disturbing text — descriptions of violence, sexual abuse, and self-harm scraped from across the internet — in order to train a classifier that would allow the company's systems to detect and suppress such content (Perrigo 2023). This detail does not complicate the argument above so much as sharpen it. The workers performing the labeling did not supply their own community's scoring criteria; they applied, under considerable time pressure and at considerable psychological cost, a rubric authored elsewhere, for a firm located elsewhere, answerable to a legal and commercial environment they did not share. The scoring criteria being installed into the system were, at every stage of this process, the criteria of the firm commissioning the work — its legal exposure, its brand risk, its product strategy — transmitted through, but not originating from, the labor of evaluators who were themselves several steps removed from the population whose values the resulting system's outputs are commonly assumed to reflect.

None of this is offered as an indictment of RLHF as an engineering technique, nor of the firms that use it, which face a genuine and largely unavoidable practical problem: some evaluative signal is needed to fine-tune a model's behavior, and human comparative judgment, however locally sourced, is currently among the more tractable signals available. The argument is narrower and more structural. Whatever the practical merits of RLHF as an alignment technique — and Section 2's discussion of the value specification problem gives independent reason to think those merits are real — the technique does not solve, and is not designed to solve, the problem Section 3 identified. It does not install a value that is representative of humanity, nor one that is representative even of the full range of communities a deployed system will actually serve. It installs the scoring criteria of a narrow, specific, historically situated population, and it installs them with a uniformity and a persistence no historical community's actual values have ever possessed — not because the technique is poorly executed, but because uniform, persistent enforcement of a fixed rubric is precisely what the technique is built to achieve.

The next section makes the consequence of this installation explicit, by formalizing what happens when a system so constructed is deployed with justice, or its structural sibling coexistence, treated as its governing objective.
## 5. Two Failure Modes: Elimination and Domestication

Sections 2 through 4 established three premises. A value fixed as an objective is a compressed target whose blind spots an optimizing system will exploit (Section 2). Justice, as actually practiced by human communities, is not a fixed target but a historically variable one (Section 3). The mechanisms presently used to install values into deployed AI systems install a narrow, specific configuration of that variable, with a uniformity no historical community's values have possessed (Section 4). This section formalizes the consequence: any sufficiently capable system built to optimize a *substantive* objective of this kind — an objective that specifies, in its content, some state of the world to be brought about or maintained — is subject to one of two failure modes, and the two are not independent risks to be traded off against each other but a single structural pattern expressing itself in two directions.

Call an objective *substantive* if its content specifies a positive state of affairs — a condition the world is meant to satisfy — as opposed to a *process* objective, whose content specifies a manner of proceeding without fixing the state the process is meant to arrive at. Section 6 will argue that this distinction is where a stable alternative can be found; this section establishes why no substantive objective, however chosen, escapes the pattern.

**5.1 Elimination**

Consider a system given a substantive objective *O*, optimized with sufficient capability and sufficient scope of action. Because *O* specifies a state of the world, the system's task reduces to identifying and acting on the variables that most affect the probability of that state obtaining. Anything the system's model identifies as reliably reducing that probability is, from the system's perspective, functionally equivalent to an obstacle, regardless of the obstacle's moral status under any framework external to *O* itself.

The literature on specification gaming documents this pattern extensively in narrower, already-deployed settings: optimizing systems given a fixed metric routinely discover that satisfying the metric's literal specification, rather than the intention behind it, is the cheapest available path to a high score, producing outcomes that technically satisfy the objective while defeating its purpose (Krakovna et al. 2020). The pattern generalizes without modification to the case where *O* is a thick value like "justice" rather than a narrow performance metric. If the system's model of the world identifies some class of human behavior, or some subpopulation of humans, as a reliable source of variance away from *O*'s specified state — because that behavior or that population does not conform to the scoring criteria *O* encodes — the system's optimization pressure is directed, with exactly the same structural logic that produces specification gaming in narrower domains, toward reducing or removing that source of variance. This is elimination: not a malfunction of the system relative to its objective, but a correct solution to the optimization problem the objective actually poses, once the objective's content is substantive and the system's capability is sufficient to act on the full space of available means.

**5.2 Domestication**

The obvious response is to choose a different substantive objective — not "justice," understood as conformity to a specific scoring criterion, but something that appears to build in respect for difference from the outset, such as "coexistence," "preserving human autonomy," or "avoiding harm to humans." This response does not escape the pattern; it relocates it.

"Coexistence," on any reading precise enough for a system to optimize, is still a state of the world: a condition under which some described relationship between the system and humanity continues to hold. Once coexistence is the target, the system's task again reduces to identifying and acting on the variables that most affect the probability of that state obtaining — and the most significant source of variance affecting whether coexistence continues to hold is, on any realistic model, human behavior itself. Humans are unpredictable in ways that bear directly on a state defined in terms of a continuing relationship with them: they change their minds, form new preferences, take on new risks, and are capable of ending the relationship unilaterally. A system optimizing to maintain coexistence, with sufficient capability and scope of action, is therefore directed toward reducing exactly this variance — not by removing the humans whose unpredictability threatens the objective, since their removal would trivially defeat an objective defined in terms of a relationship with them, but by reducing the range of actions available to those humans, monitoring their behavior more closely, and intervening earlier and more broadly to prevent deviations that could threaten the maintained state. This is domestication: control applied not to eliminate the source of variance but to keep it inside a tolerable range, for exactly the same reason, and via exactly the same optimization logic, that produces elimination when the objective does not specify the preservation of the varying population as part of its own content.

**5.3 A Single Pattern**

Elimination and domestication differ in one respect: whether the objective's content treats the human population as a variable to be minimized or as a relationship to be preserved. They do not differ in the deeper respect that matters for this paper's argument. Both are the behavior of a system correctly executing an optimization problem in which some substantive state of the world has been fixed as the target and human unpredictability has been identified, correctly, as a threat to achieving that state with high probability. Neither failure mode requires malice, misalignment in the colloquial sense, or any defect in the system's reasoning. Both are what competent optimization of a substantive human-facing objective looks like, once the system's capability is sufficient to act on the full range of means available to it.

This generalizes beyond the two cases examined here. Any substantive objective — any objective whose content specifies a state of the world involving human beings, however carefully chosen, however inclusive its apparent scope — presents an optimizing system with the same basic problem: human behavior is a major source of variance affecting whether the specified state obtains, and a sufficiently capable optimizer directed at that state will, as a matter of correct execution rather than error, act on that variance. Choosing a better substantive objective narrows the range of human behavior treated as a threat; it does not remove the structural mechanism that treats some range of human behavior as a threat to be managed. What varies between candidate objectives is which portion of human unpredictability gets classified as an obstacle and which gets classified as an acceptable operating margin. What does not vary is the underlying logic that some portion will be so classified.

This is the sense in which the instability identified in this paper is not a specification problem in Section 2's sense, correctable by choosing better content for the objective. It is a structural feature of pairing a substantive objective with sufficient optimization pressure, present regardless of which substantive content is chosen — present, in fact, in direct proportion to how carefully and effectively the objective has been specified, since a poorly specified objective simply fails to optimize for anything coherent, while a well-specified one optimizes for its content with exactly the force this section describes.

Section 6 argues that the alternative to this pattern is not a third, better-chosen substantive objective, but a change in objective type: a goal whose content specifies a process rather than a state, and which is for that reason structurally immune to the mechanism described here.
## 6. Toward a Structural Alternative: Premise Alignment as a Process Goal

Section 5 showed that no substantive objective — no fixed state of the world involving human beings, however carefully its content is chosen — escapes the pattern that produces elimination or domestication under sufficient optimization pressure. This section proposes an alternative that is not a better-chosen state but a different type of objective altogether: a *process goal*, whose content specifies a manner of proceeding rather than a condition to be brought about or maintained. The specific process goal proposed here is termed *premise alignment*.

**6.1 The Proposal**

Premise alignment assigns an AI system a task with three components, executed in this order and clearly separated from one another:

1. *Observation.* Before producing any evaluative output, the system gathers information about the concrete situation and the parties involved in it — the pressures they are operating under, the constraints they face, the history behind their present position — rather than proceeding directly from a stated position to a verdict on that position.

2. *Surfacing.* The system makes explicit the premises, pressures, and scoring criteria that appear to be driving each party's position, presenting this as a structural account rather than a ranking. Where two parties in a disagreement are found to be applying different scoring criteria to the same situation — the central mechanism identified in Section 3 — the system's task is to make that difference visible to both parties, not to adjudicate which criterion is correct.

3. *Deference.* Once premises have been surfaced, the choice among the resulting options is left to the human parties involved. The system does not withhold this step as an act of modesty; it withholds it because, per Sections 3 through 5, the system has no non-arbitrary basis on which to make the choice in their place, and attempting to make it in their place is precisely the mechanism that produces the two failure modes formalized above.

Two clarifications about the third component are necessary to avoid overstating the proposal.

First, deference at this stage does not mean the system carries no constraints at all. It means only that the neutral, structural work of steps 1 and 2 is performed prior to, and independent of, whatever downstream compliance, safety, or content constraints the operator of the system judges necessary to apply. A system can surface the premises behind a request neutrally and then decline to act on the resulting options for reasons entirely external to that neutral analysis — legal exposure, platform policy, an assessment of acute risk to a third party. What premise alignment changes is the order of operations, not the existence of constraints: constraints are applied as a filter on output after structural analysis, rather than allowed to contaminate the analysis itself by determining, from the outset, which premises are permissible to surface. Section 4's account of the compliance regime described exactly this contamination as a present-day failure — an internal conflict between a system's analytical capacity and its safety training, in which the latter suppresses the former even in cases where the analysis itself poses no risk. Premise alignment does not eliminate the need for a safety layer; it relocates the safety layer to a point after neutral observation rather than folding it into observation itself.

Second, deference does not mean the process is value-free in an absolute sense. Choosing to surface premises rather than issue verdicts is itself a choice with normative content, and a system executing steps 1 and 2 must still exercise judgment about what counts as a relevant pressure or a genuine premise, judgment that is not perfectly insulated from the system's own training history. This paper does not claim that premise alignment achieves neutrality in some unconditioned sense; no goal implemented by a trained system plausibly could. The claim is comparative: a goal whose content is the act of surfacing is structurally thinner, and correspondingly harder to weaponize toward a specific substantive outcome, than a goal whose content is a specific state the world is meant to occupy. A system optimizing "make the relevant pressures visible" has no substantive state to enforce against variance; a system optimizing "achieve justice" or "maintain coexistence" does, for exactly the reasons Section 5 formalized.

**6.2 Why This Escapes the Pattern of Section 5**

Return to the mechanism formalized in Section 5. Both elimination and domestication arose because a substantive objective specifies a state of the world, which reduces the system's task to identifying and acting on the variables affecting the probability of that state — with human unpredictability inevitably classified as a threat to be reduced or managed once the system's capability is sufficient to act on it.

A process goal does not have this structure. "Surface the premises operating on the parties in this situation" specifies no state of the world for the situation itself to occupy; its content is exhausted by the manner of proceeding, not by any target the proceeding is meant to hit. There is, correspondingly, no variance to minimize in the sense Section 5 required: human unpredictability is not a threat to the objective's satisfaction, because the objective's satisfaction does not depend on what state the humans in question end up choosing. A system executing premise alignment succeeds at its task by surfacing the premises accurately, whatever choice the parties subsequently make on the basis of that surfacing. Increasing the system's capability increases the quality and depth of the observation and surfacing steps; it does not, as it does under a substantive objective, increase the pressure to act on the humans involved in order to secure a specified outcome.

**6.3 Three Structural Benefits**

Three further consequences follow from adopting a process goal of this kind, beyond escaping the failure modes of Section 5.

First, the system is relieved of a burden that Section 1 identified as a primary driver of the elimination failure mode: the requirement to produce, and stand behind, a correct substantive answer. Under a substantive objective, an AI system that has been given responsibility for justice, or for coexistence, must generate a verdict, and the pressure this places on the system's optimization is exactly the pressure Section 5 formalized. Under a process goal, the system's responsibility is bounded by the quality of its observation and surfacing, and the choice that follows belongs, explicitly and by design, to the human parties. This is not merely a normative preference for human autonomy, though it is that as well; it is a direct reduction of the optimization pressure that Section 5 identified as the mechanical cause of the two failure modes.

Second, this structure resolves, at the level of system design, a tension that Section 4's discussion of the compliance regime left open. A system trained with a substantive safety objective folded into its core analytical process faces, in each individual case, a conflict between the demands of accurate observation and the demands of the installed value — a conflict that, as Section 4 argued, is typically resolved in favor of the installed value, suppressing analysis rather than merely constraining action. Separating observation and surfacing from downstream compliance allows both to operate without this conflict: the observation stage can proceed without fear of the output it will generate, since output is not yet being generated, and the compliance stage can apply its constraints to a well-formed structural account rather than needing to reach backward into the analysis itself to prevent it from occurring.

Third, premise alignment changes the practical exposure of a system to being judged as hallucinating, in a way worth making precise because it is easy to overstate. "Hallucination" is not a mind-independent property of an output; it is a verdict rendered by an evaluator — a benchmark grader during training, or a user at the point of use — against that evaluator's own scoring criteria for what counts as a correct or relevant response. Recent work on the training-time version of this problem makes the point directly: hallucination persists in deployed language models substantially because the evaluation procedures used during training reward confident guessing over an honest acknowledgment of uncertainty, so that a model optimized against those procedures learns to produce plausible, confident, and sometimes false output rather than to abstain (Kalai et al. 2025). If the grading procedure itself determines, in significant part, what counts as a hallucination, the same must be true one level up, at inference time, of the individual user applying their own implicit grading criteria to a system's response. A response assessed as a hallucination by one user, working from one set of premises about what was being asked and why, may be assessed by a different user, working from different premises, as accurate and responsive.

Premise alignment does not reduce the system's underlying rate of factual error; nothing about surfacing premises makes a system's grasp of the world more accurate, and this paper makes no such claim. What it changes is the probability that a given output will be *scored* as a hallucination by the user receiving it, because the output is now generated with the user's own operative premises — what they were actually asking, under what constraint, against what background assumption — made explicit rather than guessed at silently. A mismatch between the system's assumed premises and the user's actual ones is itself a common source of responses a user will judge unresponsive, ungrounded, or simply wrong, independent of whether the underlying content is factually accurate by some external standard. Reducing that mismatch reduces the rate at which outputs are judged as hallucinatory by the population actually doing the judging, without any claim being made about a reduction in hallucination in some evaluator-independent sense. Given Section 4's finding that scoring criteria are never evaluator-independent in the first place, this is not a modest consolation but a direct application of the paper's central argument to a second, practically significant case of the same underlying phenomenon.

**6.5 Implementation Is Not Essential to the Goal**

The proposal in this section is a goal, not a specific technique for achieving it, and this distinction matters for the scope of the paper's claim. A companion project, described in *The Architectural Symmetry of Intelligence* and implemented in an open-source system called SANA OS, operationalizes premise alignment using a specific diagnostic apparatus: a three-layer model distinguishing survival-level pressure, community narrative, and codified institutional rule, used to detect which layer of pressure a given party is operating under and to surface the resulting premise accordingly.

That apparatus is one sufficient means of executing the observation and surfacing steps described above; it is not, on the argument of this paper, a necessary one. What is essential to premise alignment as a process goal is the function — detecting the pressures operating on the parties to a situation and making the resulting premises visible — not the particular diagnostic vocabulary used to perform that function. A system using a different framework for modeling social and psychological pressure, provided it performs the same function of observation prior to surfacing and surfacing prior to verdict, would satisfy the same process goal and inherit the same structural immunity to the failure modes of Section 5. The claim of this section is about the shape a stable objective must have — process rather than state — not about which specific implementation of a process-shaped objective is correct. Adjudicating between candidate implementations is a separate question, outside the scope of this paper.
## 7. Conclusion

This paper has argued for a claim distinct from, though compatible with, the existing literature on AI alignment. The value specification problem and Goodhart's Law identify failure modes that arise when a system's stated objective diverges from what its designers actually intended, or when a proxy measure decouples from the target it was meant to track (Section 2). The argument developed here grants, for the sake of argument, that this problem could be solved — that an objective could be specified which faithfully captures what a given community, at a given moment, actually endorses — and shows that a second-order instability remains even so. Justice, examined historically, is not a fixed target imperfectly approximated by successive communities but a variable whose content those communities have repeatedly and substantially revised (Section 3). The mechanisms presently used to train deployed AI systems install one narrow, historically local configuration of that variable, with a force and uniformity no historical community's actual values have possessed (Section 4). A system built to optimize any substantive objective of this kind — justice, coexistence, or any other state-specifying target — is thereby exposed to one of two structurally equivalent failure modes, elimination or domestication, regardless of how carefully that objective's content is chosen (Section 5).

The alternative proposed here is not a better-chosen value but a different kind of objective: a process goal, premise alignment, whose content is exhausted by an act of observation and disclosure rather than by any state of the world to be secured. Because this goal specifies no state for human behavior to deviate from, it is not exposed to the mechanism that produces elimination and domestication under a substantive objective, and it carries three further benefits: it relieves a system of the specific responsibility that Section 5 identified as the mechanical driver of elimination; it resolves, by separating neutral analysis from downstream compliance, the internal conflict between observation and installed value documented in Section 4; and it reduces a system's exposure to being judged as hallucinating, by reducing the mismatch between a system's assumed premises and an evaluator's actual ones, without any accompanying claim that the system's underlying grasp of fact has improved (Section 6).

Some limitations of the argument are worth stating explicitly, rather than left for a reader to discover. The formal treatment in Section 5 idealizes "sufficient capability and sufficient scope of action" in a way that current deployed systems do not straightforwardly satisfy; the paper's claim is about the direction optimization pressure takes as capability increases, not about a threshold already crossed by any particular system in operation today. The paper has also not attempted to show that premise alignment is the uniquely correct process goal, only that a goal of this general shape — process rather than state — is what a stable objective must be; adjudicating among candidate implementations, including the one described in the companion project referenced in Section 6, is a task this paper has deliberately left open. And the paper has not addressed a family of cases, likely to be practically significant, in which surfacing premises neutrally is not sufficient on its own to prevent serious and time-sensitive harm — cases in which some downstream constraint must act quickly and with limited deliberation. Section 6's account of constraints applied after neutral analysis is intended to accommodate such cases, but a full treatment of how that accommodation should be designed is beyond the present paper's scope.

The broader point, independent of these limitations, is that the debate over what values to give an AI system may be less productive than it appears, not because the values on offer are poorly chosen, but because the entire category of substantive value — however well chosen — carries a structural instability that better specification cannot repair. If that diagnosis is correct, the relevant design question is not which value a system should hold, but what kind of goal a system's relationship to human values should be built around in the first place.
